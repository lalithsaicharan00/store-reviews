### ⚠️ Eight warnings before any number here is used

1. **250 reviews is a small corpus, and one review is 0.4%.** A single review clears "Weak signal"; three clear "Meaningful". Findings resting on 1–3 reviews are leads to investigate, not measured rates, and this report says so in the sentence rather than only in the band. The findings this corpus can carry on its own are the ones above ~20 reviews: design praise, ease of use, reported outcomes, developer-support praise, and the free-tier habit cap.
2. **Exactly one storefront reaches the 50-review threshold: the US, at 58 (23.20%).** Every other storefront in Part 6 is labelled **[limited evidence]** and carries no standalone conclusion. Brazil (31) and Russia (29) are close enough to be interesting and far enough below to be unsafe; they are reported as leads.
3. **This corpus is extremely positive and that is itself a finding to be careful about.** 213 of 250 reviews (85.20%) are 5★, and 202 of 250 (80.80%) contain no complaint and no request at all. The written mean 4.612 sits *below* the public averages (US 4.89 over 287 ratings), which is the normal direction, but the gap is small. **The 37 reviews carrying any negative code (14.80%) do almost all of the diagnostic work in this report.**
4. **The free-tier habit cap changed at least twice during the corpus, and the corpus records both changes.** Reviewers name the number they were allowed: **3** until 22 Dec 2024, then **1** from 23 Dec 2024 through January 2025, then **3** again from Nov 2025. Mean rating in those three windows runs 4.848 → 4.293 → 4.725. This is the single most consequential thing in the corpus and §0.1 and §7.2 treat it as such.
5. **11 reviewers state the developer gave them premium for free after they asked, and all 11 are 5★.** Ten of the eleven are US. This is a real and unusual customer-success practice, but it is also a **review-integrity issue**: a cohort of 5★ reviews written by people who had just received something free. §1.7 discloses it, §0.4 analyses it, and every headline finding is re-run with that cohort removed (§1.7) — none changes band.
6. **There is no version field on any review.** Every cause-and-effect claim here (the cap change, the 1.0.8 dip) is inferred from review text and dates plus the external version history, which itself says nothing: **all 21 release notes for this app are the identical string "Bug fixes and performance improvements."** The inference is shown each time; it is never presented as a logged fact.
7. **138 reviews (55.20%) are not in English**, across 12 languages. All were read in the original; translations here are the analyst's and are marked. Machine translation was not used.
8. **Half the corpus was written in the app's first four and a half months.** 126 of 250 reviews (50.40%) fall in 2024-11-15 → 2025-03-31, and 2025-01 alone holds 51. Anything in Part 7 about "recent" behaviour rests on the 50 reviews of 2026.

# PART 0 — READ THIS FIRST

**Executive summary in one line:** Habit Hearts is an unusually well-liked aesthetic habit tracker whose product is its visual design (152 reviews, 60.80%) and whose only significant, repeated and rating-destroying problem is the size of the free habit allowance — a number the developer appears to have set to 1 for six weeks over Christmas 2024, taking the mean rating from 4.848 to 4.293 and producing 13 of the corpus's 20 one- and two-star reviews, then quietly reverted.

## 0.1 The free habit cap is the whole negative story, and the corpus dates the change

`PAY_FREE_CAP` = **24 reviews (9.60%, High-priority, mean 2.12★)** — the lowest-rated theme of any size in the corpus, against a corpus mean of 4.612. It is the only negative theme above 4%.

22 reviewers state the number of habits they were allowed for free. Sorted by date, they fall into three clean windows:

| Window | Period | Reviews | Mean ★ | 1–2★ | 1–2★ share | `PAY_FREE_CAP` | Cap numbers named |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| A — free cap reported as **3** | 2024-11-15 → 2024-12-22 | 33 | 4.848 | 0 | 0.0% | 2 (6.1%) | 3×3 |
| B — free cap reported as **1** | 2024-12-23 → 2025-01-31 | 75 | 4.293 | 13 | 17.3% | 14 (18.7%) | 1×13, 2×1 |
| C — free cap reported as **3** again | 2025-02-01 → 2026-09-03 | 142 | 4.725 | 7 | 4.9% | 8 (5.6%) | 3×4, 4×1 |

The numbers are not ambiguous: **every "1" report falls between 23 Dec 2024 and 3 Jan 2025, and nobody reports 1 before or after** (the single "2" report is 19 Jan 2025). Inside those twelve days, thirteen separate reviewers on six storefronts — `br`, `ca`, `es`, `gb`, `nl`, `us` — say 1.

- `12097520784` (us, 2★, 23 Dec) — *"This app is free but I literally can only make one habit before it corners me into paying."*
- `12105105356` (es, 1★, 25 Dec) — *"La versión gratuita solo te deja poner 1 hábito, entiendo que no lo dejen ilimitado, pero con solo un hábito me parece inservible."* ("The free version only lets you set 1 habit. I understand not making it unlimited, but with only one habit it seems useless to me.")
- `12124750581` (br, 1★, 30 Dec) — *"um limite de 6 hábitos já estava Ok e reteria muito mais usuários no app de vocês"* ("a limit of 6 habits would have been fine and would retain far more users").
- `12130425530` (us, 1★, 1 Jan) — *"Why download a habit tracker app for the purpose of tracking a single habit?"*

And the same window is where the ratings are:

- **Window A (cap 3): 33 reviews, mean 4.848, 0 one- or two-star reviews.**
- **Window B (cap 1): 75 reviews, mean 4.293, 13 one- or two-star reviews — 17.3% of the window.**
- **Window C (cap 3 again): 142 reviews, mean 4.725, 7 one- or two-star.**

Overlaying the external version history (§2.2) puts version **1.0.8 (shipped 2024-12-18)** across the start of window B; reviews written while 1.0.8 was current average **3.80 over 35 reviews**, the lowest of any release window in the corpus.

**What this supports, and what it does not.** It supports the claim that *the free allowance is the dominant driver of this app's bad ratings*, and that *three is survivable while one is not*. It does not prove the developer changed a server-side value in 1.0.8 — there is no version field on reviews and the release notes say nothing. But thirteen independent reviewers across six storefronts do not misread "3" as "1" within twelve days of each other, and none of them says "1" before or after. See §7.2.

## 0.10 The seven decisions this corpus supports

1. **Never set the free habit cap to 1 again.** Window B is the experiment and it is already run: mean 4.848 → 4.293, 13 one- and two-star reviews in six weeks. (§0.1, §7.2)
2. **Test 3 against 5–6 rather than against 1.** Three still draws 8 complaints in window C, and `12124750581` names six as the number that would have retained him. (§5.4)
3. **Fix the paywall interstitial before touching the cap.** Two reviewers describe the upgrade prompt as an "ad" that appears on every entry and blocks feature taps. (§3.3 N2)
4. **Give free users reminders.** `11976106713` reports notifications themselves are paywalled — for a habit tracker that makes the free tier not a smaller product but a different, broken one. (§3.3 N3)
5. **Fix the water preset and the time-of-day ordering.** Both are in `13903773093`, both are correctness bugs, and one corrupts data the user cannot repair. (§0.8, §3.3 N5)
6. **Make the Russian storefront purchasable, or make the grant path explicit there.** Five `ru` reviews, four in 2026, describe an unbuyable app; the developer is already granting access one email at a time. (§0.5, §6.4)
7. **Keep the grant programme, and instrument it.** It produces 11 five-star reviews and real loyalty, but it is currently invisible in the product: three more reviewers ask for it publicly because they do not know how. (§0.4, §5.6)

## 1.2 Schema actually present

All 13 fields are present on all 250 records, with **zero empty titles, bodies, authors or country names**:

`review_id` · `app_id` · `app_name` · `country` · `country_name` · `rating` · `title` · `body` · `author` · `date` · `vote_count` · `vote_sum` · `is_edited`

`date` is ISO-8601 UTC to the second. `is_edited` is **false on every record** — no review in this corpus has been edited. `vote_count` is non-zero for **9** records (3.60%), all of them `1`; far too sparse to weight anything by, and not used. `country_name` carries a human name for 28 storefronts and the bare ISO code for 8 (`KH`, `PA`, `LV`, `EC`, `SV`, `CR`, `BY`, `KZ`); those eight are named from the ISO code where used and flagged. Bodies arrive HTML-escaped; the pipeline unescapes before any reading or matching.

**Fields that do not exist in this corpus and therefore cannot be analysed:** app version per review, device, developer response, helpfulness ranking beyond raw `vote_count`. Every statement in this report about *when* something changed is inferred from review text and dates, or taken from the external listing — never from a version field, because there is not one.

**Every review in the corpus was read individually, in its original language, in date order, in ten chunks of twenty-five** (`Temp/72-chunks/001.txt` … `010.txt`). No sampling, no keyword pre-filter, no truncation at a context limit. The theme assignment for each review was written by hand while reading (`Temp/72-cls/001-125.txt` and `126-250.txt`, keyed by working index 1–250), then resolved to `review_id` through `Temp/72-index.tsv` and asserted field-by-field against `reviews.jsonl` by `Temp/72-build-classification.py`.

The 40 storefronts probed that returned nothing are: `ae al ao at ba be bg bs bw cn co cz dk do dz gr gt hn hu in jp kg lu md mg mn mz ni py qa ro rs sa sg sn sz tr uy uz ve`. Their absence is a real fact about this app's reach — **it has no written reviews in Japan, China, India, Turkey, Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Saudi Arabia or the UAE** — and is used in §6.7, not silently dropped.

## 1.4 Deduplication rule

**No records were deduplicated.** There are **zero exact `(title, body)` duplicate pairs** and **zero repeated review bodies**. The denominator for every global percentage in this report is therefore 250.

One author name appears twice: **`Evorita` (es) posted `12051634060` on 2024-12-11 and `12052507920` on the same day**, both titled "Útil y preciosa", both 5★, the second longer and adding the competitive framing and the price judgement. Both are retained and both are counted, because they are distinct records on the storefront and the second contains information the first does not. They are flagged `META_DUP_AUTHOR` so any theme they both carry can be re-checked at n−1. At n−1: `TRK_HEALTH` and `TRK_TIMEOFDAY` fall from 2 to 1, `TRK_COUNT` 5→4, `TRK_PRESET` 4→3, `ECO_CROSSSELL` 8→7 (Very strong → Meaningful), `PAY_PAID` 12→11, `COMP_BEST` 24→23, `DES_AESTHETIC` 99→98. **No finding in this report depends on counting that pair twice**; the only band change is `ECO_CROSSSELL`, which is a context code and not a recommendation.

## 1.5 Processing method

1. `72-dump.py` — unescape, sort by `(date, review_id)`, assign working index 1–250, emit `72-all-reviews.txt`, `72-chunks/`, `72-index.tsv`.
2. Read all ten chunks in order, in full. Record one line per review in `72-cls/` (`index CODE,CODE` plus a free-text note). The codebook grew while reading; codes introduced late were applied backwards by re-reading the affected reviews.
3. `72-themes.py` — the codebook: **86 codes in 14 families**, plus **16 composite unions**. Definitions in Appendix B.
4. `72-build-classification.py` — resolve index → `review_id` against `reviews.jsonl` and emit `72-review-classification.py`. Asserts on any duplicate index, out-of-range index, undefined code, repeated code within a review, or unclassified record.
5. `72-validate.py` — integrity gate (§1.3 last four rows, plus: zero undefined codes, zero unused codes, all union members defined, rating distribution matches manifest). Output at `72-validate-out.txt`.
6. `72-aggregate.py`, `72-extra.py`, `72-checks.py` — counts, unions, ratings, storefronts, market groups, eras, language, integrity, and targeted verification of every narrative claim made in Parts 0 and 8.
7. `72-tables.py` — generates every table in this report into `72-gen/`, plus `_facts.json` (680 facts).
8. `72-build-report.py` fills the numeric placeholders and refuses to write if any placeholder is unresolved; `72-verify-report.py` re-checks the written file against the corpus (§1.8).

**Classification is entirely manual.** No automated clustering, no keyword rule, no model-assigned label was used to place a review in a theme. Keyword search was used only in `72-checks.py`, and only to test **negative** claims (§0.6: "no review mentions AI") where a false negative would be the dangerous error. The keyword matcher folds Latin diacritics but preserves Cyrillic, so Russian and Ukrainian text is searched in its own script.

## 1.6 Limitations — read these before citing any figure

1. **Size.** 250 reviews. One review = 0.40%. Three reviews clear "Meaningful"; eight clear "Very strong". Where a finding rests on 1–3 reviews this report says so in the sentence.
2. **One eligible country.** Only the US (58) meets the 50-review threshold. Brazil (31), Russia (29), France (16), the UK (15) and Germany (14) are reported as **[limited evidence]** with no standalone conclusions.
3. **Selection.** The 250 written reviews are 21.5% of the 1161 public star ratings across the same 36 storefronts. The other ~78% rated without writing and are unrepresented here. Brazil is the sharpest case: 161 public ratings, 31 written reviews, public average 4.90 against a written mean of 4.74.
4. **The grant cohort.** 15 reviews (6.00%) are written by people who state or strongly imply they received something free from the developer, and all are 5★. Every headline is re-run without them in §1.7. No band changes, but the corpus mean does (4.612 → 4.587).
5. **Half the corpus is the launch quarter.** 126 of 250 reviews fall in the first four and a half months. Any "the app is now …" statement rests on the 50 reviews of 2026.
6. **No version field, and release notes that say nothing.** All 21 releases carry the identical note "Bug fixes and performance improvements" (external, §2.2). The 1.0.8 overlay in §0.1 and §7.2 is an inference from dates, presented as one.
7. **Language.** 138 reviews (55.20%) are non-English across 12 languages; 10 records are written in a language other than their storefront's default (e.g. `12177323617` on `lv` in Russian, `12970447080` on `ua` in English). All were read in the original.
8. **External data is a snapshot.** Prices, version, languages and rating counts were read on 13 Sep 2026 and describe the app *now*, not at the time any given review was written — which matters most in §0.1, where a December 2024 paywall cannot be verified against a 2026 listing.
9. **Rating is not preference.** A `neg` code inside a 5★ review is still negative (§4.7); 13 five-star reviews carry a negative or request code.

### N2 — The upgrade prompt behaves like an ad — `PAY_PAYWALL_NAG` = 2 (0.80%), Emerging, mean 4.00★

Two reviews, so a lead, not a rate — but both describe the same mechanic and one of them is the corpus's most detailed negative review.

- `11976106713` (ca, 3★, 2024-11-21) — *"if you don't pay at least $10, most of your screen is taken up by a huge ad encouraging you to upgrade the app, and nearly every feature you click brings you back to this ad."*
- `12239882971` (us, **5★**, 2025-01-28) — *"I love the widgets but I just wish we didn't get an ad to buy premium every time we enter the app."*

The second is the diagnostic one: a five-star user, three months later, still describes a full-screen upsell **on every app entry**. For an app whose entire value proposition is a pleasant daily ritual (§0.2), an interstitial in front of that ritual is a direct attack on the thing people are paying — or not paying — for. Note also that two reviewers praise the *absence* of ads (`PAY_NOADS`, 2): the paywall is being experienced as the advertising this app otherwise does not have.

### N3 — Reminders may be paywalled — `PAY_FREE_NOTIF` = 1, and `BUG_NOTIF` = 2

One review says notifications are behind the paywall (`11976106713`, ca, 3★, Nov 2024) — *"you can't even get notifications for them"*. Two more report notifications simply not working:

- `13066857850` (ru, 3★, 2025-08-27) — *"приходят не все уведомления о привычках"* ("not all habit notifications arrive"). This is the corpus's only rating reduction caused by a plain defect.
- `14363105130` (ie, **5★**, 2026-07-29) — titled *"Notification bug"*; resolved by the developer (§0.3).

Together `U:reminders_any` = 10 (4.00%), of which 7 are positive. Small numbers, but the direction matters: **a habit tracker whose free tier cannot remind you is not a smaller product, it is a different one.** Treat `11976106713` as the highest-value single review in the corpus to verify against the current build.

## 5.5 Post-purchase problems, refunds, cancellation

Three of the 12 payers report a problem (segment rate 25.0%):

- `12112764195` (ca, 2★) — accidental purchase; **the purchase is not recognised after delete-and-reinstall** and the app asks her to buy it again (`BUG_RESTORE`).
- `12163311892` (ua, 2★) — **refund refused** (`PAY_REFUND_DENIED`).
- `12143331423` (de, 4★) — minor defects (`BUG_MINOR`, `UX_SLOW`, `UX_UNDO`), not a purchase problem.

**No reviewer in this corpus reports a recurring charge, a renewal, or a cancellation**, which is consistent with the one-time model people believe they bought (§2.3).

## 5.6 The grant programme as a monetization instrument

§0.4 covers the evidence. The product question it raises:

- It **works**: 11 grants → 11 five-star reviews, several of them long, specific and evangelical (`12384692708`, `12163624657`, `13936621361`).
- It is **invisible**: three further reviewers ask for it *in public* (`13450460890` asks for a discount; `12251848448` describes it second-hand) and at least two more — `12112764195` (*"I'm to tight for money"*, 2★) and `12124750581` (price objection, 1★) — are exactly the population it exists for and evidently did not know.
- It is **concentrated**: 10 of 11 grants are US. Either the route is only discoverable in English, or it is only being asked for in English. Russia, where payment is structurally impossible (§0.5), produced one grant and four unhappy reviews.

**The cheapest available experiment in this whole report:** make the hardship route discoverable inside the paywall itself, in every language the app already ships. The corpus contains a 2★ and a 1★ that would plausibly have been 5★ if it had been.

## 6.7 What country data does *not* support

- **No claim about Japan, Korea, China, India, Turkey, Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Saudi Arabia or the UAE.** All were probed; all returned zero written reviews. For an app shipping 27 languages including Japanese, Korean, Simplified and Traditional Chinese, Thai and Vietnamese, the absence of a single written review in any Asian storefront except the Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia, Cambodia and Kazakhstan is a real distribution fact, and it is the largest gap between what the app is localised for and where it is used.

## 7.4 The five trends this corpus supports

### T1 — The free-cap complaint spiked and receded — **an event, not a trend**

`PAY_FREE_CAP` runs 12.70% → 4.05% → 10.00% across the eras, which looks like a U. It is not: 14 of the 24 complaints fall in the six-week window B, and removing that window leaves a flat ~5–6% baseline. The E3 uptick is five reviews, two of which are Russian payment failures counted here because the reviewer concludes the free tier is useless (§0.5). **The cap is a persistent low-level tax on ratings with one catastrophic spike.**

### T2 — Competitive preference is rising, strongly — **rising**

`COMP_BEST` 5.56% → 10.81% → **18.00%**; `U:competitive_pos` follows. Nearly one review in five written in 2026 says this is the best habit tracker the writer has used, against one in eighteen at launch. The people still arriving in year two are arriving having tried the alternatives. This is the single most positive trend in the corpus.

### T3 — Reviews are getting shorter and less informative — **rising**

`META_LOWINFO` 23.81% → 27.03% → **36.00%**, while `U:requests_any` goes 7.14% → 8.11% → **0.00%**. Not one review written in 2026 asks for a feature. **Do not read that as product completeness** — read it as a corpus that is increasingly made of one-line enthusiasm. It is the main reason Part 3's request backlog is thin and the main reason §7.5 refuses several trend claims.

### T4 — Purchase friction is rising — **rising, on small numbers**

`U:purchase_friction` 1.59% → 1.35% → **8.00%**; `PAY_REGION_BLOCK` 0.00% → 0.00% → **6.00%**; `U:bugs_any` 1.59% → 4.05% → **8.00%**. This is four Russian reviews plus one, so the numbers are small — but the mechanism behind them (§0.5) is external, dated and verifiable: operator billing in Russia stopped working in March 2026, and `13936621361` and `14053933246` both name that month independently. **This is a real 2026 regression and it is not the app's fault, which does not make it less of a revenue problem.**

### T5 — Ease of use overtook raw design praise as the second thing people mention — **rising**

`UX_EASE` 15.87% → 27.03% → 24.00% while `U:design_praise` stays flat at ~60%. Early reviewers reacted to the look; later reviewers, who have used it longer, report that it is also easy. Nothing in the corpus says the app changed; the likelier reading is that E1 is full of `META_EARLY` reviewers (11 reviews, mostly in E1) rating a first impression.

## 7.5 What persisted, and what this corpus cannot say

**Persisted, flat across all three eras:** design praise (61.11% / 60.81% / 60.00%), reported outcomes (46.03% / 39.19% / 38.00%), unspecific praise (26.19% / 29.73% / 28.00%). The core proposition has not moved.

**Refused trend claims:**

- *"The app got more reliable"* — `U:bugs_any` **rose** 1.59% → 8.00%. But that is 2 → 4 reviews and E3's defect reports come disproportionately from one unusually thorough Russian reviewer. No claim either way.
- *"Support got better/worse"* — `U:support_praise` 23.02% → 12.16% → 24.00%. The dip is E2, which is also the era with the fewest grants. This tracks the grant programme, not support quality.
- *"Monetization complaints are falling"* — `PAY_PRICE_HIGH` falls 4.76% → 2.70% → 2.00%, but that is 6 → 2 → 1 reviews and the fall is entirely explained by window B ending.
- *"The audience is shifting female/ADHD"* — `DES_GIRLY` 2.38% → 8.11% → 6.00% and `OUT_ADHD` 1.59% → 1.35% → 4.00% both rise, but on 3–6 and 1–2 reviews respectively. Recorded as a hypothesis for §8.5, not as a trend.
- **Nothing about 2026-09** — the corpus ends on 2026-09-03 with one review that month.

## 8.4 Positioning and monetization strategy

**The free tier is an acquisition instrument, and it is currently priced as a monetization instrument.** The corpus contains 24 complaints about the cap and **zero reviewers who say the cap made them buy** (§5.2). Every stated purchase trigger is something else: trust from another app in the family, the design at first sight, a sale price, or wanting to support the developer. That is an unusual and useful position — it means the cap is currently doing almost none of the work it exists to do, while causing all of the rating damage.

**The ecosystem is the strongest asset that is not the design.** `U:ecosystem` 26 reviews, rising to 14.00% in 2026, and `ECO_CROSSSELL` shows it converts: three of the twelve stated payers bought on the strength of Fleur or Floret. Two reviewers ask for the *next* app unprompted. Cross-promotion inside the family is better evidenced here than any in-app feature.

**"Lifetime, one payment" is what people repeat.** 7 reviews praise it explicitly and nobody in the corpus describes a subscription renewal. The listing sells a Monthly and a Yearly; the corpus suggests the Lifetime SKU is what this audience wants and talks about. The two different Lifetime prices in the listing ($9.99 and $15.99) and the two different prices quoted to one Russian reviewer in one session are a coherence problem worth closing (§2.2).

## 8.6 Research questions this corpus cannot answer

1. **Did the cap actually change, and who changed it?** The corpus establishes what users saw; it cannot see the config. Check the remote-config history against 2024-12-18 and 2025-02.
2. **What does the ~78% who rate without writing think?** 250 written reviews against 1161 public ratings. The written corpus is 21.5% of the signal.
3. **Why zero reviews in Japan, Korea, China, India or Turkey** for an app localised into all of those languages? That is a distribution question, not a review question.
4. **Does the grant programme pay for itself?** 11 grants produced 11 5★ reviews. Whether those reviews produced more revenue than the grants cost is not answerable from text.
5. **Is the E3 request drought real?** Zero feature requests in 50 reviews could be product maturity or could be shorter reviews (§7.4 T3). In-app feedback would separate them.
6. **What happened to widgets between the listing and the reviews?** 9 reviewers praise them, one counts "more than 50", and the store description does not mention widgets at all.

## 8.7 What to do first

If only three things happen: **(1) hold the free cap at ≥3 and treat that number as a rating-sensitive setting, not a growth lever; (2) take the upgrade interstitial off app launch; (3) make the hardship route visible inside the paywall in every language.** Items 1 and 2 address the source of every one-star review in this corpus. Item 3 costs a screen and converts the exact population that currently writes them.

# PART 9 — EVIDENCE APPENDIX

## A. Counting rules

1. **Denominator.** Every global percentage uses **250**, the full corpus. No review is excluded from any global denominator for any reason — not low information, not non-English, not the duplicate-author pair, not the grant cohort.
2. **Non-exclusive themes.** A review may carry several codes; 897 assignments over 250 reviews, mean 3.59 per review. Percentages within a family therefore sum to more than the family total.
3. **Unions count each review once.** `U:` codes are set-unions over member codes; a review carrying three members contributes 1 to the union.
4. **Signal bands** are applied to the share of the **full corpus**, never to a sub-denominator — except in §6.4, where the US denominator (58) is used and stated in the table header, because the US is the one storefront that clears the 50-review threshold. Where a sub-denominator is used elsewhere (the 12 payers, a rating band) the figure is explicitly labelled a **segment rate**, both denominators are shown, and the band is not restated.
5. **Mean ★ per theme** is the unweighted mean rating of the reviews carrying that code. At n = 1–3 it is quoted for completeness and carries no statistical meaning.
6. **Direction** (`pos` / `neg` / `req` / `fact`) is a property of the **code**, not of the review carrying it. A `neg` code inside a 5★ review is still negative — see §4.7.
7. **Dates** are the review's own `date` field, UTC, truncated to the day. Eras are defined in §7.1; the cap windows in §0.1 are a separate, disclosed split.
8. **Review IDs** are the source `review_id` verbatim. Every ID printed anywhere in this report is verified present in `reviews.jsonl` (§1.8).
9. **External facts** (price, version, release dates, languages, public rating counts) come from the iTunes Lookup API and the App Store US web listing accessed 13 Sep 2026, are labelled at every point of use, and are never mixed into a review-derived count.
10. **Translations** are the analyst's, made from the original text, and are always shown next to the original. Machine translation was not used.

## C. Judgement calls and known error risk

Every borderline decision made while classifying, so a reader can re-derive or overturn any of them:

1. **`CAPFACT_n` records what the reviewer says, not what was true.** A reviewer who misremembers would be counted. The defence is that thirteen independent reviewers on six storefronts said "1" inside one twelve-day window and nobody said it outside, in either direction, across twenty-two months — but the codes remain reports, not measurements. §0.1 and §7.2 are written accordingly.
2. **`PAY_FREE_CAP` is assigned to `14053933246` and `14309961093` (both ru)**, which complain about not being able to *pay* and conclude the app is useless without the paid tier. They are also `PAY_REGION_BLOCK`. Removing them from `PAY_FREE_CAP` takes it from 24 to 22 (8.80%) — still High-priority, and §0.1's window analysis is unaffected because both are in window C.
3. **`PAY_GIFTED` requires an explicit statement of receipt.** Three reviews that read like grants but do not say so are *excluded*: `12058580930` (*"the creator of the app did something amazing for me"*), `13836013164` (*"Thank you so much for the help"*), `12251848448` (describes the programme; coded `PAY_GIFT_PROGRAM`). Two Russian reviews thanking the developer for "access" (`12842688096`, `13324237749`) are coded `PAY_ACCESS_THANKS`, a weaker claim. If all six were counted as grants, `PAY_GIFTED` would be 17 (6.80%) rather than 11. **This is the most consequential judgement call in the report** and §0.4 uses the conservative number.
4. **The `Evorita` pair is counted twice.** Rationale and n−1 sensitivity in §1.4. One band changes (`ECO_CROSSSELL`); no finding depends on it.
5. **`12245253288` is retained despite paraphrasing the store listing.** It is the sole source of `SYNC_GOOD`, so §0.6's iCloud observation is phrased as an absence of complaint, not as praise. Flagged `META_COPY_LISTING`.
6. **`12314349514` (es) is written entirely inside quotation marks** and reads like promotional copy, but it contains specific, checkable product detail (clear charts, subtle reminders, customisation) that matches other reviews. It is counted normally and noted here.
7. **`11976106713` is coded `PAY_FREE_NOTIF`, a distinct code from `BUG_NOTIF`.** "You can't even get notifications for them" describes gating, not failure.
