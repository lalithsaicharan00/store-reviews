### Five things to know before using any number here

**1. Every review was read individually, in full, in its original language.** The corpus is 105 records — small enough that no sampling, keyword classifier or clustering step was used. Each review was hand-coded against a taxonomy built from the reading itself. Because of this, §11 can print a **complete per-review index: all 105 IDs with their assigned themes**. Any number in this report can be traced back to the exact reviews that produced it.

**2. The corpus is 91.43% one-star.** 96 of 105 reviews are 1★, 8 are 5★, 1 is 3★. There are **zero 2★ and zero 4★ reviews**. A distribution with no middle is not a normal product-feedback distribution, and it changes what this corpus can be used for: it is a **dispute record**, not a satisfaction survey.

**3. The public rating is 2.13 stars higher than what people write.** The US listing shows **3.45★ from 259 ratings** (accessed 10 Sep 2026) against a **written-review mean of 1.324**. 105 of those 259 ratings carry text; the other 154 do not. Nothing here is a statement about the average user — only about the population motivated enough to type.

**4. The dominant subject is not the app.** 75 of 105 reviews (71.43%) raise at least one billing, pricing, cancellation or refund theme. Only 10 of 105 (9.52%) report a functional defect in the software. This is unusual for a habit tracker and is the single most important fact in the report.

**5. All eight 5★ reviews were posted inside one nine-day window.** 23–31 December 2025, all from the US storefront, all 44–74 characters long. §5.2 sets out the observable facts and the competing explanations. Treat the 5★ band as **evidence of uncertain provenance** until that question is resolved.

## Executive summary

**Wisey: Habit Builder is not, in its review corpus, a habit-tracking product. It is a payment funnel with a habit tracker attached, and the reviews are a dispute record.**

The nine findings that matter, each linked to its evidence:

1. **Billing happens outside the App Store, and that single architectural choice generates most of the corpus.** 18 of 105 reviewers (17.14%) state the subscription does not appear in their Apple subscriptions. The developer's own terms confirm two purchase paths and that website purchases are non-refundable. → §0.1
2. **A second subscription for e-books is charged on top of the first**, with its own cancellation path that several reviewers say does not work. 11 of 105 (10.48%), across five storefronts, 8 months apart. → §0.2
3. **Money moves without authorisation in two distinct ways**: 19 of 105 (18.10%) charged after cancelling; 19 of 105 (18.10%) charged with no subscription they recognise. → §0.3
4. **Refunds are refused (25 of 105, 23.81%), and three specific mechanics turn refusal into a fraud allegation**: a requirement to prove 14 days of use, an advertised money-back guarantee that is not honoured, and retention offers in place of an answer. → §0.4
5. **The save-flow destroys price credibility.** Customers charged $98.50/month and $99 were offered $5 and $49 lifetime plans to stay. All three who report it left 1★, and one states the offer told him the real value. → §0.5
6. **Cancellation is obstructed by five named, reproducible mechanics** — email-only cancellation, bold/small button asymmetry, ~10 confirmation steps, split cancellation across two subscriptions, and loss of account access mid-flow. 24 of 105 (22.86%). → §0.6
7. **Under the billing problem is a product ceiling.** 13 of 105 (12.38%) say the phone's alarm, Notes, calendar, paper or Excel does the same job for free. A checklist cannot carry a $45–$99 price. → §0.7
8. **The positioning creates the sharpest risk.** 13 of 105 (12.38%) accuse the product of targeting people with ADHD *because* they will forget to cancel; 6 escalated to banks, card networks and the FTC. When you sell to people who struggle to follow through, cancellation friction reads as exploiting the deficit you claim to treat. → §0.9
9. **All eight 5★ reviews were posted in one nine-day window in December 2025**, all US, all 44–74 characters, one containing a homoglyph. Their provenance is unresolved and every positive figure in this report is stated with and without them. → §5

**What is genuinely worth taking from this corpus** (§10.3): one reviewer found real value in a web course library the app never links to; the defect surface is tiny (10 reports in 16 months); and two departing customers named the competitors they moved to — one of whom described *"customer service aren't trying to rob you"* as a product feature.

| ID | Charged | Offered on cancellation |

## 1.4 Processing method

1. **Read every record.** All 105 reviews were printed in full, date-ordered, and read individually — original language preserved, nothing truncated, nothing skipped for being short, old, low-information or non-English.
2. **Build the taxonomy from the reading**, not before it. 63 theme codes emerged; every code is defined in §12.1.
3. **Hand-code each review.** Each of the 105 IDs was assigned its codes manually. The coding table is a literal `review_id → [codes]` map, so §11's per-review index is generated from the same object that produced every count in this report. There is no second source of truth to drift from.
4. **Assert coverage programmatically.** The code map's key set was asserted equal to the corpus ID set, and each review's code list was asserted duplicate-free, before any counting ran.
5. **Aggregate.** All counts, percentages, per-country tables, star-band tables, era tables and ID lists were computed from that map. No figure in this report was typed by hand from memory.
6. **External checks last**, and kept separate: Apple's iTunes lookup API for listing facts, the App Store page, the developer's published subscription terms, and a web search for third-party corroboration. All are marked as external where used and listed in §12.4.

**Why no classifier.** At n=105 a keyword classifier would add precision/recall error without adding throughput. The trade-off is the opposite one: these counts carry **judgement error** (one analyst's reading) rather than **matching error**, and they are not floors in the way keyword counts are — a theme count here is the number of reviews in which one reader judged the theme present.

## 1.5 Limitations and judgement calls

- **n=105 is small.** One review is 0.95%. A theme with 3 reviews clears the "Meaningful" threshold on 2.86%, which at this sample size is fragile. Every count under 5 should be read as directional.
- **Selection bias is extreme and visible.** 91.43% 1★ against a 3.45★ public listing. People with a working habit tracker do not write; people fighting a charge do. Nothing here estimates prevalence among users.
- **The 5★ band's provenance is unresolved** (§5.2). All positive quantities in this report are reported twice where it matters: including and excluding that band.
- **"Confirmed payer" is a conservative judgement.** It requires a first-person statement of having paid, been charged, subscribed, cancelled a subscription, or requested a refund on a charge. Three reviewers who strongly imply payment without stating it (`12874978864` `13202910675` `13754537415`) are coded `INFERRED_PAYER` and are **excluded** from the 64-payer denominator.
- **Amounts are as reported by reviewers.** Currency is frequently unstated; `13280826420` and `13369386593` explicitly say USD from Canada. No amount was converted, and no total was computed across reviewers.
- **No version data**, so no finding can be attributed to a release, and no regression can be dated to one.
- **Only one storefront (US, n=72) clears the 50-review bar.** Every other country conclusion in §8 is explicitly labelled limited evidence.
- **Reviews reflect the reviewer's understanding of what happened to them**, not verified transactions. "Charged without consent" means the reviewer did not believe they had consented. Several such reports are consistent with a trial that auto-converted per terms the reviewer did not read — which is a product-design finding either way, but not proof of unauthorised charging.
- **External sources describe the product as it stands in September 2026**; the corpus spans May 2025 – August 2026. Where the two conflict, the review is evidence about its own date, not about today.

# PART 5 — THE FIVE-STAR BAND: PROVENANCE

This section exists because every positive number in this report depends on whether these eight reviews are organic. It reports observations, not conclusions.

## 5.1 What is observable

| Observation | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Time window** | All 8 fall between **23 and 31 December 2025** — a 9-day span inside a 441-day corpus |
| **Exclusivity** | These are the **only** 5★ reviews in 16 months; there is no other 5★ before or after |
| **Storefront** | 8 of 8 are US |
| **Length** | 44–74 characters (mean 57.1) vs a 1★ mean of 284.2 and median of 197 |
| **Posting hour (UTC)** | 6 of 8 fall in a 07:01–11:18 band; the other two at 08:47 and 09:08 — all 8 within 07:01–13:00 |
| **Register** | Generic category praise ("Great app", "So good", "ok"); 5 of 8 end without terminal punctuation |

| **Context** | The same 9-day window also contains two 1★ reviews (`13546645984`, `13564772884`), so the window is not a period when the store was only accepting positive reviews |

## 5.2 The two explanations, and what would settle it

**Explanation A — organic.** A cohort of satisfied users happened to review over the Christmas holiday. Consistent with: the burst falls in a natural downtime period; two of the eight name real features that exist (widget, reminders).

**Explanation B — inorganic.** The band is the product of an incentivised or purchased review campaign. Consistent with: 100% concentration in 9 days of 441; 0 five-star reviews in the other 432 days; uniform brevity at 20% of the 1★ median; a narrow morning-UTC posting band; generic register; and the homoglyph in `13572140998`.

**No single observation proves either.** Taken together the pattern is a substantially better fit for B than for A, but this report does not have access to device, account or purchase metadata, and **does not assert that the reviews are inauthentic.** What it does assert is that the eight reviews cannot bear analytical weight: they name no mechanism, describe no use case, and are the sole source of every positive count in the corpus.

**What would settle it:** Apple's own review-integrity signals; whether the same author handles reviewed other Koflimin apps in the same window; and whether Wisey's other three apps (which carry 4.08★, 4.07★ and 3.03★ against this one's 3.45★) show similar dated clusters.

**How this report handles it:** every positive finding is stated with and without the burst. Excluding it, the corpus is 97 reviews at a mean rating of **1.021**, containing three positive statements (§3.3).

| 7 | **Reply to support email within the cancellation deadline, or move cancellation out of email entirely** | §7.1 mode 3 · `13597970945` | A 24-hour cancellation deadline served by a 48-hour support queue is unsatisfiable by construction. |
| 8 | **Retire the discount ladder.** Replace a $5-lifetime save-offer against a $99 charge with a plain refund or a pause | §0.5 (3) | The offer is currently teaching customers that the list price is a 20× markup, in public, in writing. |

## 10.2 The transferable lesson — for anyone building in this category

This is the part of the analysis that survives outside Wisey.

**a. Trust is a purchasable feature, and this corpus prices it.** `12863798573` recommends a competitor on the grounds that its "customer service aren't trying to rob you." In a category where the software is commoditised, **Apple-billed subscriptions, one-tap cancel, a visible price on the paywall and a renewal reminder are product differentiators**, and they cost almost nothing to ship. Say so in the store listing.

**b. Selling to people who struggle with follow-through raises the bar on cancellation, it does not lower it.** 13 reviewers (12.38%) make the same argument: friction that a neurotypical user calls annoying, this audience calls predatory — because the product's own marketing asserts they cannot be relied on to complete multi-step tasks. **The ADHD positioning and the retention funnel are incompatible.** Pick one. If the positioning stays, the cancellation flow has to be the *easiest* flow in the product.

**c. A checklist has a free substitute pre-installed on the device.** 13 reviewers name it: alarm, Notes, calendar, paper, Excel (§0.7). Any habit product charging above a few dollars must deliver something a checklist structurally cannot — genuine personalisation, real coaching content, accountability with another human, or data the user could not assemble themselves. **Wisey charged $45–$99 for the substitute and got 96 one-star reviews.**

**d. A guarantee is a conversion lever that only works once.** Three reviewers bought *because* of the money-back guarantee (§6.2) and all three escalated when it was refused. The guarantee's value is in the buying decision; its cost is in honouring it. A guarantee you will not honour is a fraud allegation you have pre-purchased.

**e. Do not let the funnel promise what the product does not contain.** Five reviewers were sold a personalised plan and received an empty tracker (§0.8); one was sold in Spanish and delivered an English-only app (§8.7); several were sold a programme and received four thin apps. **Every one of these gaps was created by marketing, and every one was paid for by the product's rating.**

## 10.3 The one product opportunity in the corpus

`13398540937` (ca, 3★) is the only reviewer who found the product's actual value, and they found it **by accident**:

> "I didn't realize that the online portal/website has tons of helpful info. I thought it was just the wisey apps… Perhaps advertise the website on the apps when they open?"

Read against §0.7 and §2.1, this is a complete diagnosis. The tracker is a commodity; the course library is the differentiated asset; and **the app never mentions the library exists.** Every reviewer who called the product "just a checklist" (13 of them) may have been describing the 10% of the product they were shown.

**The action is a one-screen change**: surface the course library from the app's first open, with a link, as this reviewer literally requests. **The experiment worth running** is whether reviewers who reach the portal rate differently from those who never do — this corpus has exactly one such reviewer, and they are the only person in sixteen months who wrote something constructive.

## 10.4 Experiments worth running

| Experiment | Hypothesis | Success measure |
|---|---|---|
| In-app course-library entry point on first open | Reviewers who see the content asset rate materially higher than those who see only the tracker | 3★+ share among users who open the library ≥1× |
| Genuine free tier (track 1–3 habits forever) vs paywall-on-open | The 4 no-trial refusals (§6.4) represent a larger silent population | Paid conversion and 30-day retention vs current |
| Apple-billed IAP vs web checkout, same price | Off-store billing is causing the dispute volume, not the price | Refund rate, chargeback rate, 1★ share per cohort |
| Refund-on-request within 30 days, no conditions | Refund cost < dispute + reputation cost | Chargeback rate, review mean, support ticket volume |

## 10.5 Research questions this corpus cannot answer

1. **Are the eight December 5★ reviews organic?** (§5.2) Requires review-integrity signals this corpus does not contain. Every positive finding here is contingent on the answer.
2. **What is the actual refund rate?** 25 refusals and 1 partial refund among 105 reviewers says nothing about the denominator of all refund requests.
3. **Do App Store IAP buyers ($6.99 / $29.99) complain at all?** **Not one of the 105 reviewers clearly identifies as an Apple-billed purchaser.** If the IAP cohort is genuinely quiet, that alone settles recommendation 10.1.1.
4. **Did the price rise, or did the plan mix shift?** (§9.3) Needs plan-level data; the corpus has no version or plan field.
5. **Why did review volume collapse in 2026 H1?** (§9.6) Cut acquisition spend, complaints redirected off-store, or a genuine fix — the corpus is consistent with the first two and not the third.
6. **Does the ad creative differ by market?** (§8.4) Five non-US and zero US reviewers report a promised personalised plan; one Chilean reviewer confirms ad language differs from app language.

## 12.1 Counting rules

- **Denominator is 105** for every global figure; the storefront's own n for country figures; the era's own n for era figures; **64** for payer-segment rates; **96 / 8 / 1** for the 1★ / 5★ / 3★ bands.
- **Themes are non-exclusive.** A review reporting a double charge, a refused refund and a thin product is counted in all three. Percentages sum to well over 100%.
- **No deduplication was applied.** All 105 `review_id` values are unique, no `(title, body)` pair repeats, and no `author` repeats. No record was merged, dropped or collapsed.
- **A review carries a theme only if this analyst judged, from reading the full title and body, that the reviewer asserts it.** No keyword rule was used; no automated match was accepted without reading.
- **`CONFIRMED_PAYER` requires a first-person statement** of paying, being charged, subscribing, cancelling a subscription, or requesting a refund on a charge. Implication is not enough — three such reviewers are coded `INFERRED_PAYER` and excluded from the 64.
- **Segment rates** (e.g. "32.8% of payers") are always labelled as segment rates and always shown beside the global count. **They never substitute for the §3.2 threshold classification, which always uses the global denominator of 105.**
- **Star-band means** are computed over the matched reviews only.
- **Amounts are quoted as the reviewer wrote them.** No currency conversion, no cross-reviewer totals.
- **The safety exception in the thresholds table was invoked once**, for `13636589290` (§0.3), a single review below the normal promotion floor.
