### ⚠️ Three warnings before any number here is used

**1. The corpus is very small (38). One review is 2.63%.** Every theme in this report crosses the "high-priority" line at 3 reviews. **The counts are the honest unit; the percentages are a formality the method requires.** No finding here has the statistical weight of a large corpus, and none should be treated as a measured rate.

**2. But the reviews are unusually substantial.** Median body length is **236 characters**, mean **346** — roughly four times the density of the small corpora elsewhere in this dataset, and only 3 of 38 reviews are under 60 characters. Several single reviews (`8557538403`, `9289248731`, `7886496270`) contain more usable product detail than a hundred one-line reviews. **This corpus is thin in count but rich in content**, so it supports qualitative mechanism findings far better than it supports rates.

**3. No country qualifies for individual analysis, and none is attempted.** Threshold is 50 reviews; the largest storefront is the US at **18**. Per the analysis direction for this app, **global findings only** — no per-country section. All 38 reviews are pooled. Market and language observations, where material, are flagged inline as **limited evidence**.

# PART 0 — READ THIS FIRST

## 1. This is not a habit-tracker corpus. It is a relaunch-migration corpus.

**20 of 38 reviews (52.63%) — high-priority signal — reference the app's predecessor, "Daily Routine Classic."** More than half of everything written about this app is written in comparison to a version that no longer exists.

The history the reviews establish: an earlier paid Daily Routine app built a devoted following, went years without maintenance, became unusable, was pulled from the App Store, and was then relaunched in September 2021 as this new app on a subscription model.

That legacy base splits into two populations with almost no overlap, and the gap between them is the largest single fact in this corpus:

| Population | n | % of 38 | Mean ★ |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Returning users delighted by the relaunch** | 13 | 34.21% | **4.46** |
| **Legacy buyers who feel betrayed by it** | 6 | 15.79% | **1.17** |

**A gap of 3.29 stars, inside the same user base, over the same event.** What separates them is not the product — both groups describe the same app — but whether they had paid for the original.

## 2. The relaunch bought roughly one quarter of goodwill, then spent it

| Era | Window | n | % | Mean ★ | Rating spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Relaunch euphoria** | 5 Sep – 31 Dec 2021 | 10 | 26.32% | **4.30** | 5★6 / 4★3 / 1★1 |
| **2. First full year** | 2022 | 13 | 34.21% | **2.62** | 5★3 / 4★2 / 2★3 / 1★5 |
| **3. After the final update** | ≥ 9 Jan 2023 | 14 | 36.84% | 2.93 | 5★5 / 4★1 / 3★1 / 2★2 / 1★5 |

The drop from Era 1 to Era 2 is **1.68 stars**, and it is the clearest movement in the corpus. Era 1 is almost entirely returning classic users celebrating (9 of 10 reference the classic version). Era 2 is when new users — who have no nostalgia and hit a paywall on first launch — begin to arrive.

**Interpretation, labelled as such:** the relaunch's opening ratings were a loan against the classic app's reputation, not a measure of the new product. When that base was exhausted, the rating fell to what the new app earns on its own merits.

## 3. The single largest negative theme: you cannot try the app at all

**7 reviewers (18.42%) — high-priority signal** report being unable to use the app without paying first. Mean rating **1.14** — 6 of the 7 are 1★, the lowest-rated theme in the corpus.

- `8863711662` (ca, 1★): *"Was excited to use this just to find out that I can't because it's not free??"*
- `10621025077` (us, 1★): *"As soon as I opened the app I couldn't even use it without a pop up to pay for subscription…"*
- `10024637302` (us, 2★): *"i opened the app for the first time and I couldn't get out of the payment subscription page if there is a solution please reply"*
- `12167506108` (ca, 1★): *"It's just a scam. You have to sign up for a subscription before you can try the app."*
- `9992175230` (in, 1★): *"Without even giving a free version or showing how the app works, They are casually asking for a huge amount for signup."*
- `9471017050` (us, 1★): *"Dont download it requires a monthly subscription and costs 35-90 depending on your sub didnt even use it one time."*
- `9289248731` (us, 1★): *"the free trial is entirely unusable without going through payment confirmation. Can't even look at the settings or examine how the app will handle cancelation."*

**None of these seven describes the product.** They rated an app they never saw. The store label reads **"Free · In-App Purchases"** (listing, 9 Sep 2026), which is what sets the expectation these reviewers arrive with.

`9289248731` names the specific mechanic that turns a paywall into a trust problem: a user who cannot inspect the cancellation flow before committing payment details has to trust the developer blind — and this developer's classic app had already been abandoned once.

## 4. The pricing is the second trust problem, and the numbers are now public

The listing (9 Sep 2026) shows three options:

| Option | Price |
|---|---|
| Monthly Subscription | **$1.99** |
| Annual Subscription | **$19.99** |
| **One-Time Purchase** | **$64.99** |

**5 reviewers (13.16%) — high-priority signal** object to the price, mean **1.60**. `9289248731` (us, 1★) makes the comparison that stings: *"A one-time purchase is available for three times the price of a premium word processor or music workstation. But don't forget, all future versions are free. Until the next pricing model, that is."*

The arithmetic reviewers are reacting to: **$64.99 is 3.25 years of the annual subscription.** For an app whose predecessor was abandoned after a one-time purchase, asking $64.99 for "lifetime" access is asking users to bet 3+ years of subscription value on the developer's longevity — from a developer who has already broken that bet once. `7849996761` (us, 1★) asks it in one sentence: *"Is there a chance that I buy the new version and they aren't going to drop it in couple years again?"*

**That question has now been answered by events. See §5.**

**5 reviewers (13.16%) — high-priority signal** object specifically to the shift from one-time purchase to subscription, mean **1.40**. The angriest is `8874399635` (se, 1★): *"you broke functionality and ditched support for the old 'classic' version I bought; to make this version — that costs considerably more to purchase up-front, forcing people to instead 'subscribe'… That is disgusting."*

And `9289248731` makes a specific, serious allegation about the classic app's final state: *"Developer appears to have deliberately broken the earlier version of this app, so that it 'crashes' when you attempt to add an item to your schedule… There is a special dialog that pops up: 'Crashing in 5 seconds.'"*

**Treat that as a reported user perception, not an established fact.** One reviewer describes it; the corpus contains no corroboration of intent, and a deliberate crash dialog is equally consistent with a developer-facing debug string left in a build. It is recorded because a paying customer publicly concluded the developer sabotaged a product they had bought — which is a reputational fact regardless of what the code actually did.

## 5. The app was abandoned once, relaunched on a subscription, and has now been abandoned again — while still charging

**This is the most consequential finding in the corpus.**

The listing shows the current version is **1.1.4, released 9 January 2023** (accessed 9 Sep 2026) — **more than three and a half years without an update**, on an app that charges a monthly subscription.

**4 reviewers (10.53%) — high-priority signal** name this directly, and they are the corpus's most recent substantive records:

- `13193213543` (cy, 2★, 27 Sep 2025): *"Loved this app, but it's abandoned… it hasn't been updated in over 2 years and now has bugs and crashes. Please update, it's too good to be left like this!"*
- `13441826130` (us, 3★, 26 Nov 2025): *"This app maps out time so beautifully. I really enjoy it, but **I'm getting tired of the paywall with zero app updates.** If we can get iPad and watch apps along with more regular debugging, I will keep paying. Otherwise, I am switching to Blocos when they develop a calendar integration."*
- `13120304447` (de, 4★, 10 Sep 2025): *"I feel it is still in the beta. And I hesitate to buy/subscribe. The price of one-time purchase is too expensive for the app with many bugs."*
- `14384355988` (ru, 1★, 3 Aug 2026 — the final review in the corpus): *"Obsolete. Where's the updates? I want my Daily Routine Classic back, please."*

The corpus closes on a user asking for the abandoned app back, because the replacement has been abandoned too. **`13441826130` states the churn condition in plain terms — continued payment is contingent on continued development — and names the competitor and the exact feature (calendar integration) that will trigger the switch.** That is the single most actionable review in the corpus.

**`7849996761`'s 2021 question — "are they going to drop it in a couple of years again?" — was answered in the affirmative, by the release history, in public.**

## 6. What people actually love: the timeline, and one feature no competitor has

Against all of that, the product's core is admired.

**9 reviewers (23.68%) — high-priority signal** state that no alternative exists, mean **4.22**. This is an unusually strong differentiation signal: nearly a quarter of the corpus says they searched for a replacement and failed.

- `7988825337` (pl, 5★): *"at some point it disappeared from App Store, and I was searching for routine/habits app that would fill the void, but honestly none of apps could."*
- `8013032927` (us, 4★): *"I've spent the gap between classic and the return of the new version searching for an alternative. I had no luck."*
- `7775035126` (ru, 5★): *"I've been using it for 10 years, even when it degraded to a buggy app. It was buggy but it was still the best and the only one perfect for my needs."*

**8 reviewers (21.05%) — high-priority signal** praise the timeline/time-blocking model itself, mean **4.50**. `9504102829` (us, 5★) reaches for a clinical analogy: *"The only app in the store that ques up duties as the time approaches… Epic, a major health care app for doctors and all caregivers, has a Brains timeline. This is what Daily Routine has achieve[d], but with a lot of adjustability and flexibility."*

**The one capability named as unique to this app is global schedule shifting** (`7886496270`, us, 5★ — 1 review, 2.63%, meaningful signal, but high-value):

> *"there is no other app available that allows you to easily globally shift a schedule, rather than moving all tasks individually. This has proven invaluable for my erratic work and sleep schedule. It's also great if you need to adjust your schedule gradually due to jet lag or sleep problems."*

The same review benchmarks the one competitor that came close and explains why it failed: *"Sorted was the only other one I found that allowed shifting an entire schedule, but it collected tasks if you didn't check each one off daily, then froze up and required constant management."*

**This is the product's moat, it is stated once, and it appears nowhere in the store listing.**

Two more value signals worth naming:

- **Time blindness / ADHD.** `8557538403` (us, 4★): *"I experience time blindness and this app helps manage that immensely."* One review (2.63%), but it identifies a specific clinical use case for a timeline-first scheduler.
- **Support that converts.** `8244932708` (us, 5★): *"I asked for a feature and the dev emailed me personally when they implemented it. This is above and beyond. This thing is a work of art."* The only support interaction in the corpus, and it produced a 5★.

## 7. The product is hard to learn, and the corpus says exactly which words confuse people

**7 reviewers (18.42%) — high-priority signal** describe a learning curve or missing guidance, mean **3.43** — this theme spans 2★ to 5★, so it is friction, not rejection.

**3 reviewers (7.89%) — high-priority** name the absence of a tutorial explicitly (`8013032927`, `8644242601`, `8827339965`).

`8827339965` (us, 2★) provides an unusually precise onboarding brief by listing the exact vocabulary that failed:

> *"what is a sequence? What is a block? What is an activity? What's the difference between them?… Why is it so difficult to move anything around? This app could be GREAT if it just added some common sense things."*

`8644242601` (in, 2★) gives the time-to-abandon: *"I couldn't figure how to use it. There's no proper guide or assistance offered within the app either. I deleted it after trying to make it work for a good 30 minutes."*

**Crucially, satisfied users confirm the curve is real but surmountable** — which means the problem is onboarding, not design:

- `7866331165` (ro, 5★): *"once I've got the hang of it (**takes a day of experimenting**) I got hooked by its flexibility."*
- `7988825337` (pl, 5★): *"it has very different UI than all the other apps on the market, so you may need a few moments to catch the workflow, but once you get it you'll love it!"*
- `8557538403` (us, 4★): *"A bit of a learning curve to navigate through, but certainly learnable with a little bit of time."*

**A day of experimenting is what this product asks. A hard paywall demands payment before the user has spent that day.** Those two facts are incompatible, and together they explain the corpus better than either alone.

## 1.4 Processing method

1. Parsed all 38 records; verified count, uniqueness, duplicates.
2. Reconciled the merged corpus against the 12 per-country files, ID set by ID set.
3. **Read all 38 reviews individually in full, in chronological order** — no keyword search, no sampling, no rating filter. Given a 236-character median body, every review was read end to end; several are multi-paragraph and were re-read when coding structural complaints (`8557538403`, `9289248731`).
4. Translated the 3 non-English reviews (2 Spanish, 1 German); translations appear beside every quotation and in the appendix.
5. Assigned non-exclusive theme labels by hand as explicit ID lists, then counted by script — no count in this report is hand-transcribed.
6. Asserted that every ID in every theme list resolves to a real record and that no list repeats an ID (both pass).
7. Retrieved the live store listing for external context (§1.6).

**Classification error risk:** manual, single-reader classification — consistent but not independently validated. Judgement calls disclosed in §1.5.

## 1.5 Limitations and judgement calls

- **Sample size is the dominant limitation.** 38 reviews over 5 years and 12 storefronts. One review = 2.63%. **Every theme labelled "high-priority" here rests on 3–20 reviews.** Read the counts.
- **No country analysis is possible or attempted** (largest storefront: US, 18, against a 50 threshold); global findings only, per direction for this app.
- **Severe selection bias, and it runs in a specific direction here.** This corpus contains 41 ratings' worth of app but 38 written reviews — an unusually high write rate, which happens when a product provokes strong feeling. Both poles are over-represented: nostalgic returners and blocked non-buyers. **The quiet middle — users who paid, used it, and felt fine — is almost certainly under-represented.**
- **The two populations barely overlap.** 12 of 38 reviews (31.58%) are pure praise with no criticism (all 12 are 5★, mean 5.00); 26 (68.42%) carry at least one criticism or request (mean 2.27). There is very little genuinely mixed middle ground, which makes the 3.132 mean a poor description of any actual user.
- **Judgement call — `9244113250`** (ca, 1★) contains both a current 1★ complaint (black screen after an iOS update) and the user's own pasted prior 5★ review. It is counted **once**, at its current rating of 1★, and coded for both `legacy_joy` (from the quoted prior text) and `bugs_crashes`. Counting the historical portion separately would double-count one person.
- **Judgement call — `9289248731`'s sabotage allegation** is coded under `legacy_grievance` and `sub_model_objection`, **not** as an established product defect. See §4.
- **Judgement call — `9957602682`** (cn, 5★, body: *"support ipad icloud"*) is read as a feature request, not praise, despite the 5★. The rating and the text point in different directions; the text is what is coded.
- **`13120304447`'s 4★** is coded under `bugs_crashes`, `price_too_high` and `abandonment` despite being a positive-leaning rating — a clear example of why theme means are reported separately from ratings.
- **No version field exists** in the schema, so themes cannot be tied to releases. Version dating comes from the store listing and from review text.
- **Nothing here supports a causal claim**, except where reviewers state the mechanism themselves (e.g. `13441826130`: *"If we can get iPad and watch apps… I will keep paying. Otherwise, I am switching"*).

## 5.4 Post-purchase experience and churn

**The corpus documents a complete churn cycle among paying customers:**

- `8776556254` (us, 2★) — paid, waited, gave up: *"I kept my subscription going for a few months hoping it would approach quality of legacy app."*
- `8827339965` (us, 2★) — paid, could not learn it: *"For a subscription user face should not be this bad."*
- `13441826130` (us, 3★) — still paying, actively evaluating a competitor, condition stated.
- `13193213543` (cy, 2★) — loved it, now watching it decay.

**Churn driver #1 is non-delivery of continued development.** Three of these four cite the absence of updates or improvement, not a product defect. Users bought a trajectory and the trajectory stopped.

**Notably absent from all 38 reviews:** no refund request, no complaint about being charged after cancelling, no failed restore-purchase, no billing error. **The payment plumbing works; the value proposition behind it does not.**

**And the one support interaction in the corpus converted perfectly** — `8244932708` (us, 5★): *"I asked for a feature and the dev emailed me personally when they implemented it. This is above and beyond."* This developer, when engaged, delights people. The problem is not capability or care. It is sustained capacity.

**Early 2022 shows a developer actively shipping and personally engaging.** That capacity is gone by 2023. The corpus therefore documents not an absence of ability but its exhaustion — which points at a resourcing or business-viability problem behind every other finding in this report.

## 8.4 Research questions this corpus cannot answer

1. **Is the developer still operating?** The corpus and listing show a 3.5-year release gap; neither establishes whether the company is dormant, pivoted, or defunct. **Every recommendation above is conditional on this answer.**
2. **What is the actual free→paid conversion rate, and how many users bounce off the paywall silently?** 7 blocked reviewers are the visible tip; the rate is invisible here.
3. **Did the classic app genuinely ship a deliberate crash dialog?** One reviewer reports it (`9289248731`); nothing corroborates it. Verifiable only from the old binary.
4. **How many classic buyers migrated versus churned?** 6 grievance reviews establish that the failure exists, not its size.
5. **Would a functional free tier convert better than the current hard gate?** §5.1's 3.63-vs-1.14 gap is suggestive on n=8 and n=7 — this is the corpus's clearest A/B test candidate, and it needs real data.
