# Who Pays for Habit Apps, and Why — Audience Groups

Written by Claude (Claude Code), 7 November 2026, at the user's request: start from nothing, ignore the app and every earlier decision, and find out from both stores who the paying people are and why they pay, apart from features.

## The short answer

**1. About 9 in 10 paying reviewers never say who they are.** Of about 12,060 reviews where someone really chose to pay, about 1,040 (8–9%) give any clue about their life or identity. So every group share below is a share of the people who *said so*, not of all payers.

**2. Among those who do say, two groups are three quarters of the people, and both are about the mind.** ADHD and other neurodivergent people are 290 of 660 (44%). People with depression or anxiety are 211 of 660 (32%). Everything to do with mental health or neurodivergence together is 486 of 660 (74%).

**3. Who pays *more* isn't the same as who is *biggest*.** Compared with similar reviews of the same app, **ADHD writers are 1.35–1.54 times as likely to say they paid**, the only large group clearly above average. Parents are also above average (at least 1.2 times). Depression and anxiety is the second-biggest group but pays about the average rate (0.76–1.13). Students, teens, people quitting something, and people using the app with a partner or friends pay clearly less.

**4. There are two kinds of buyer, and they buy for different reasons.**

- **"Help me" buyers** say who they are. Their top reason by far is need: the app holds up something they can't do alone. Of the 374 named payers who gave a reason, 202 (54%) gave this one.
- **"Good tool" buyers** say nothing about themselves. Of 183 who chose to pay and named no group, 87 (48%) gave no reason and 47 (26%) simply said it was a good fit for the price. Only 1 spoke of need.

**5. The groups that need it most are also the ones that get charged by surprise.** 83 ADHD or neurodivergent reviewers (22% of that group's payment reviews) describe a charge they didn't mean to make. Forgetting to cancel is part of the condition, and they say so. Fair, plain charging is an opening here, not a detail.

## How this was done

| Step | Reviews | How |
|---|---:|---|
| All reviews collected | 1,238,784 | App Store 337,331 (70 apps), Play Store 901,453 (146 apps) |
| Non-habit apps removed | 877,278 left | 12 Play Store apps that aren't habit apps, 361,506 reviews. App Store 337,331, Play Store 539,947 |
| Machine search for people who paid, in many languages | 17,646 | Sentence-level check for "I paid", "I bought", "I subscribed" and the like; 2,931 of these also have surprise-charge words |
| **Read by hand: every payer who named a life group** | 1,298 | App Store 994, Play Store 304. All of them, no sampling |
| Read by hand: payers naming only an interest (pets, data, fitness) | 120 | Random sample, surprise-charge words left out |
| Read by hand: payers naming no group | 240 | Random sample of those with 12 words or more, surprise-charge words left out. App Store 142, Play Store 98 |
| Read by hand: non-payers, to check the word match | 408 | 24 for each of 17 groups |

So every one of the 877,278 reviews was searched by machine, and **2,066 were read by hand**. Not every review was read by hand: that isn't possible at this size, and the numbers below say which is which.

Hand reading sorted the 1,298 like this: **915 really chose to pay (70.5%), 253 were charged by surprise or mistake (19.5%), and 130 weren't payers at all (10%)**. A word search alone would have counted all 1,298 as payers. Of the 915 who chose, **660 truly belong to a group**; the rest had only mentioned the word (a friend's ADHD, a "baby" bird in Finch).

**Estimated real choosers: about 12,060.** That's the 915 read, about 800 from the interest-only payers and about 10,350 from the no-group payers, each scaled by what the samples showed.

## Which group pays more

The question here is: **among people who write a positive review and say they are in this group, how much more likely are they to say they paid?** The comparison is with reviews of the same app and the same length, so a group doesn't look better just because it uses an expensive app or writes long reviews. Only 4–5★ English reviews count, because 1★ reviews are full of surprise charges. On average 1.16% of the 317,778 such reviews mention paying.

**1.0 means average.** Each row has two numbers, because a word match also catches people who aren't in the group. The first takes the word match as it stands; the second corrects it by how often hand reading found the match true. The truth should sit between them.

Chronic illness, autistic and parent reviewers bring up money more often, yet pay at about the average rate or above.

So the money means something different in each group.

### ADHD and neurodivergent: paying for an outside brain

They pay for something that remembers on their behalf. Several say it's the first or only app they've ever paid for.

- “It reminds me of everything my ADHD-brain forgets” (HabitNow, Play Store, review `99c6ba55-ef93-4b15-a016-30ad67d44610`)
- “It’s the only app I’ve ever purchased.” (Fabulous, App Store, review `7249550944`)
- “The first app I bought in 8 years” (Productive, App Store, review `1432070093`)

The same group is hurt hardest by trials and renewals, and they see it as aimed at them.

- “They know we have adhd, they know we’re going to forget” (Fabulous, App Store, review `12640922529`)
- “The ADHD tax is high enough without another forgotten subscription.” (Fabulous, App Store, review `13532038811`)
- “has become a tax I pay for having ADHD” (Fabulous, App Store, review `11903292815`)

### Depression and anxiety: paying for gentle care

They compare the app with therapy, not with other apps, and the price feels small beside it. Some pay when they have very little.

- “We all know therapy can be expensive” (Finch, App Store, review `8596861448`)
- “I justified it saying it was for my mental health” (Finch, App Store, review `9315546854`)
- “I'm broke but I still bought the upgrade” (Productive, App Store, review `1382781344`)
- “It’s like having a therapist and a Buddhist monk for a small monthly fee.” (Fabulous, App Store, review `13433275676`)

Finch holds half of these people (105 of 211), so this group is much smaller on other apps. A soft, cared-for feeling seems to be part of what they buy.

### Severe conditions, chronic illness, disability: paying to function

Few people, but the strongest need (62–66%), and among severe conditions no one reported a surprise charge.

- “a schedule is an absolute need in my life” (Fabulous, App Store, review `6372043387`)
- “I have a TBI and this is truly helping me to cope.” (Fabulous, App Store, review `6782148386`)
- “after a massive stroke” (Fabulous, App Store, review `6942704380`)

### Parents: paying for the mental load

Parents pay for the household, not only themselves.

- “I bought the full version so I could have unlimited habits” (Productive, App Store, review `1442525489`)
- “Perfect for Mom Brain!” (Productive, App Store, review `4877219849`)
- “to see if it would be any good for my children” (Me+, App Store, review `10568183655`)

### Students and teens: want it, can't pay

They pay least, and a one-time price matters more to them than to most groups. The second and third reviews here aren't payers; they show why.

- “And I'm a broke college student so that's really saying something.” (Productive, App Store, review `1630174450`)
- “its just a little much for a full time student” (Fabulous, App Store, review `9020756169`)
- “when I told them I couldn’t afford to keep paying for it” (Finch, App Store, review `9262507001`)

### Quitting: paying rarely

“I’m happy I spent my money on this and not on alcohol” (Way of Life, App Store, review `5522819619`)

### Helpers: they spread it

Few of them, but some recommend the app to the people they look after.

- “it’s been helpful to me and my clients (social worker)” (Finch, App Store, review `14131516702`)
- “I recommend it to all my clients as well” (Fabulous, Play Store, review `3298b254-6892-40eb-bd8f-1a83719cece3`)

### The silent "good tool" buyer: about 9 in 10 reviewers

Hand reading of 240 payers with no group: 183 chose to pay. Of those, 87 (48%) gave no reason, 47 (26%) said it was a good fit, 17 (9%) hit the free limit, 11 (6%) said it worked, 10 (5%) paid to make themselves commit, 5 thanks, 4 love, 1 need, 1 one-time.

- “I used the free version of this app for about a year” (Habit Tracker, App Store, review `9470291201`)
- “I purchased the upgrade because I wanted to add more than 5 habits” (Productive, App Store, review `1381064506`)
- “I truly feel the app is worth every penny” (Finch, App Store, review `11635506146`)

Three small reasons also turn up across the reading: paying to make themselves take it seriously, paying as thanks, and spending on the pet.

- “thinking if I paid for it I’d be more motivated to use it” (Productive, App Store, review `7070602236`)
- “Now I have buy in.” (Strides, App Store, review `4074569772`)
- “Best $3 a month I have ever invested in myself” (Fabulous, App Store, review `6873568827`)
- “I bought premium not because I needed to, but to support these guys” (HabitNow, Play Store, review `be9f715c-443a-4303-aa4e-256ff6e66785`)
- “it would be rude not to pay” (Habit Tracker, App Store, review `11039492170`)
- “I’m a sucker for cute little decor things for my birb” (Finch, App Store, review `9611177696`)

## Where the money probably is

This is a rough cross-check and the weakest number here. It takes the outside revenue estimates of 21 October (low confidence) and spreads each app's estimate over that app's named payers. Finch's estimate alone is about $1.7M of the $1.93M a month covered, so Finch decides the result.

| Group | With Finch ($1.93M a month, 4 apps) | Without Finch ($228K a month, 3 apps) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Depression and anxiety | 60% | 26% |
| ADHD and neurodivergent | 31% | 48% |
| Parents | 6% | 11% |

Groups overlap, so columns can add up to more than 100%. This only says where named payers sit. It doesn't count the 9 in 10 who say nothing. **Outside Finch, ADHD is the biggest named group by far; inside Finch, depression and anxiety is.**

## What can't be concluded

- **Reviews aren't all payers.** Only people who chose to write are here, and most payers never do. Some groups may write more than others. ADHD people may be keener to explain themselves, which would raise their share without raising their real numbers.
- **"Share of named payers" isn't share of payers, or of revenue.** It's only those who said so. 44% is not "44% of paying customers have ADHD".
- **The groups depend on how apps sell themselves.** RoutineFlow is sold to ADHD people, and Finch to people looking after their mental health. Each draws its own crowd, so the groups partly reflect who was marketed to.
- **Saying a word isn't proof of identity.** Hand reading fixed this for payers, but the comparison group of non-payers was corrected from only 24 reviews per group, a rough check. Parents, developers, faith and shift work move the most because of it, so their ranges are wide.
- **Matching review length may take too much away,** since longer reviews are partly how a committed person writes.
- **The word lists are mostly English,** so non-English groups are undercounted. Only 4–5★ English reviews are in the "who pays more" table.
- **The estimate of payers who weren't read** relies on 81% of them being real choosers (from 116 reviews). If that's 7 points off, the ratios move about 9%.
- **Some hand calls were judgement calls,** and long reviews were read as excerpts around the payment and group words, not always in full.
- **Reviews don't show how much anyone paid.** "Pays more" here means more likely to pay, not a bigger amount.
- **Review share isn't market size.** Nothing here says how many ADHD people are in the market or how many would pay for a new app.
