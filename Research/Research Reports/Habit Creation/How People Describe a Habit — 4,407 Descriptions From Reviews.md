# How People Describe a Habit — 4,407 Descriptions From Reviews

The user asked for this as a document of its own. It collects every place in the review corpus where someone says, in their own words, what a habit of theirs is ("I want to read 2 chapters a week", "run 5K every day"), and sorts those words into the shapes people actually use. It is the evidence that the Round 3 New Habit design is checked against, one statement at a time: [Creating a Habit — Round 3, The User's Own Words](<Creating a Habit — Round 3, The User's Own Words.md>).

The user's points are listed in [the Round 3 checklist](<../../../iOS/Docs/Checklists/Creating a Habit — Round 3, The User's Own Words Checklist.md>). The scripts, hand codes and a row for every statement are in [`Habit Creation Evidence/habit_descriptions_round3/`](<Habit Creation Evidence/habit_descriptions_round3/README.md>).

**What the table says:**

1. **Three shapes cover 70%:** every day (30.7%), an amount a day (22.1%) and N times a week (16.2%). With several times a day (10.8%) it's 80%.
2. **People say the rhythm and the amount in one breath.** "Read 2 chapters a week", "5 miles a day", "30 minutes 5x per week". Nobody splits it into a schedule and a separate goal. The period ("a day", "a week") is part of the amount's sentence, not a calendar setting.
3. **"A week" after an amount means in total.** "100 miles weekly", "6 hours every week", "three drinks per week": 130 habits. "Each time, N times a week" is rarer (40) and always says both parts ("30 minutes 5x per week"). So the words tell which one is meant; a form can simply follow them (§3).
4. **One in ten descriptions leaves something out** that a form must fill in, and always the same thing: how often. "Walk 5,000 steps" (F18, 211 habits) and "drink water in the morning" (F17, 166) mean every day unless said otherwise. "Every 2 hours" (F16, 49) needs the waking hours.

## 1. How the statements were found

The earlier Round 2 report scanned for schedule words. This time the question was different: **how does a person say a habit**, whatever the words? So the scan looked for sentences, not keywords, and every kept sentence was read and coded by hand.

| Step | What it did | Kept |
|---|---|---:|
| `extract.py` | Sentences (20–320 characters) from 922,405 English or untagged reviews with an intent marker ("I want to", "my goal", "for example", "I track", "habit of"…) and a frequency or an amount | 8,783 |
| `extract2.py`, `pats.py` | Sentences with an activity verb within six words of an amount or a frequency, with or without an intent marker | 7,867 more |
| `merge.py` | Merged and de-duplicated; sentences with no activity at all set aside | 10,969 candidates, 5,681 set aside |
| `filter3.py` | Candidates with an everyday activity word near an amount or frequency, from the ones without (app use, bugs, prices) | 7,215 + 3,754 |
| **Hand coding** | All 7,215 read and coded (`codes/`); all 3,754 read again for habits the filter missed (`dcodes/`); the 1,114 set-aside sentences that name an activity read too (`rcodes/`) | **4,407 statements** |

Every one of the 12,083 sentences in the last row was read. A sentence was kept when it states a habit: something done, avoided or limited, with a rhythm or an amount. Sentences about the app ("I track my habits every day"), about measurements ("the watch counted 3,335 steps") and one-off events were left out.

**The code** records the shape of each habit, not the activity (`codes/README.txt`):

- **Direction:** build (4,167 statements), **L** at most (123), **Q** quit or never (82), **N** count with no target (104).
- **Rhythm:** D every day · Dn N times a day · W N times or days a week · W1 once a week · S set weekdays · I every N days, weeks or months · M1/Mn · Y1/Yn · H every N hours · T time of day only · 0 none said.
- **A:** an amount with a unit. After D, W, M, Y, S or I it belongs to that period or day ("100 pages a week" = WA). After Dn, Wn, Mn, Yn it is **each time** ("run 5K 3x a week" = WnA).
- **Suffix:** :t a time of day or clock time (453 statements) · :r a range, "3–5 cups" (100) · :x something more (284, sorted into themes in §6).

`aggregate.py` joins the codes to the sentences and checks them: 4,407 statements, 0 unknown line references, 0 invalid codes. `families.py` groups codes into the 18 shapes of the table above.

**Limits.** Reviews are not a survey: people describe the habits their app couldn't handle more often than the ones it could, so the rarer shapes (monthly, yearly, per-weekday) are over-represented compared with daily life, and every-day habits are under-represented. The counts rank the shapes; they don't estimate how many users have each. Coding was done by one reader (Claude); the parser agreement in §7 is a partial second check.

## 2. Direction: build, at most, stop, or just count

| Direction | Statements | Their words |
|---|---:|---|
| Build (do it, reach an amount) | 4,167 | all of the table above |
| **At most** (a limit) | 123 | "Like max 5 smokes per day or so." (`6473560486`) · "Don't eat more than 4 packets of crisps a week!" (`6095068067`) · "Be on Facebook at most 3h a week" (`1541987452`) |
| **Quit / never** | 82 | "do not smoke and have it set for every day." (`8a21ff41-abe1-42ac-8973-4e4d3a78d07f`) · "the feature for quitting a habit needs to allow a goal of Zero Per Day." (`8468652583`) |
| **Just count, no target** | 104 | "I want to track for instance how many times I eat sugar per day." (`10023849674`) |

People who cut down say the limit exactly like a goal, with the same periods ("3 drinks a week", "5 smokes a day"), only with "at most", "no more than", "limit", "under". So a limit is the same sentence with one word changed, not a different kind of screen.

## 3. How much: amounts and their units

1,348 statements carry an amount (`vocab.py`):

| What is measured | Statements | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Time (minutes, hours) | 480 | 35.6% |
| Drinks by volume (glasses, cups, bottles, oz, ml, litres) | 278 | 20.6% |
| Distance (km, miles, "5K") | 140 | 10.4% |
| Reading (pages, chapters, books) | 104 | 7.7% |
| Steps | 85 | 6.3% |
| Reps (push-ups, squats, sets) | 56 | 4.2% |
| Servings, meals, fruit | 43 | 3.2% |
| Cigarettes, drinks, coffees (limits) | 38 | 2.8% |
| Pomodoros, sessions | 20 | 1.5% |
| Calories | 20 | 1.5% |
| Words | 19 | 1.4% |
| Money | 11 | 0.8% |

A statement can mention more than one unit, so shares add to more than 100%.

**Which period an amount belongs to, as people say it:**

| Said | Means | Habits |
|---|---|---:|
| "8 glasses a day", "5 miles a day" | that much each day | 1,007 (F02) |
| "100 miles weekly", "2 books a month", "20 books this year" | that much in the week, month or year, in total | 174 (F06 130 · F12 26 · F15 18) |
| "30 minutes 5x per week", "meditate 2x daily for 20 minutes" | that much **each time**, and a number of times | 40 each time + N a week (F05); 40 each time + N a day (within F03); 11 on set weekdays; 7 N a month |
| "15 minutes running", "Walk 5,000 steps" | the amount only; every day is meant | 211 (F18) |

**The rule people use is visible in their words:** an amount followed by a period is for that period; an amount next to "N times" is for each time. Nobody says "a weekly goal with a daily schedule". A form that shows the sentence back ("2 chapters a week", "5 km each time, 3 times a week") needs no rule of its own.

**Ranges** (100 statements, :r): "20 – 30 min a day" (`9713883199`), "sleep duration between 7 to 9 hours" (`12738821325`), "I now walk 6-7 miles a week" (`10545628723`). The lower number is the goal in every range read: the upper number is a hope, not a target.

## 5. When: times of day and every N hours

- **453 statements (10.3%) add a time of day or a clock time** to the habit: "I wake up at 7am every day" (`7303988288`), "taking vitamins in the morning and night" (`ba91430f-9393-494d-b4d8-0b8fb5f4bae0`), "wake up every morning at 6am during the week" (`7d1f8f5f-d9fd-483b-b692-a363de0c722c`).
- **154 statements give only a time of day** ("drink water in the morning"), meaning every day at that time.
- **"Every N hours" (47 statements) is a reminder rhythm, not a count.** "I wanted to set a reminder for drinking water every 2 hours, but the app only allows one reminder per task." (`db6ac44e-1523-4de3-97dc-c154502ae955`). When a window is given it is the waking day or the work day: "every 4 hours daily… only during my wake up hours (7am-10pm)" (the X13 theme, §6).

## 7. How people expect to log it

A second question sits under the first: once a habit is described, what do people expect a tap to do? A scan of the same 16,650 extracted sentences for logging words (`logging_scan.py`, 391 hits) was read and coded by hand (`logging_codes.txt`). **167 reviews** say how they expect to log:

| Expectation | Reviews | Their words |
|---|---:|---|
| **Tap once for each one as I go, and see how far I am** ("5/8") | 57 | "I want to drink 8 cups of water a day, I should be able to check off the amount each time I do the habit." (`1383777971`) · "it should say… 5/8, that would be way more motivating" (`10464151025`) · "I'm loving checking off each time to see the progress wheel light up" (`11339667935`) |
| **Time: a timer that pauses, or add the time without it** | 41 | "I would like to read for 30 minutes a day but not in one sitting." (`3640246859`) · "add an option of adding time to my goals without the timer, like just add 1 hour" (`ab79030a-7b9d-44b3-94ea-332903d0c624`) · against timers: "Timers alone adds tons of unnecessary anxiety" (`e0bc2095-2571-446f-b2a8-5923e8a13498`) |
| **Partial counts, and going over counts** | 32 | "if I have a goal of going to the gym 4 days a week and make to the gym 5 days that week, I can't log that 5th day" (`10887365100`) · "I've set the bars low to reading 30 mins a day but I often exceed the goal" (`5125560652`) |
| **Type the exact amount** | 19 | "I should be able to enter the number here rather than just marking" (`1a3e018f-800b-4b0b-9764-bc6bf3930843`) · "I need to drink 7-8 glasses a day, but I want to record how many in a day I did it." (`26832da4-b22c-40c7-9655-d7d06f50bbdf`) |
| **A step of my own size** (+500 ml, +10) | 13 | "my bottles of water are 17oz each, so when I finish a bottle I put 17oz" (`6721564744`) · "tapping a +1 button 80 times for an 80m run is exhausting, but a custom +10 would be perfect" (`c4c14097-1651-4354-9c35-aa38a542d6e3`) · "just click the '500ml' button rather than typing in a value every single time!" (`11323727126`) · "I need the counter to go 10 by 10 instead of 1 by 1" (`0c0d9477-fd54-45fe-938c-4e07567c811f`) |
| **Undo, or minus one** | 10 | "drink 10 cups of water a day) not possible to decrement… are you serious?" (`12970071023`) · "I can't undo my 'drink 10 glasses of water' task" (`7251255831`) |
| **A limit: log each one, and show when it's over** | 6 | "tap once each time I have a cigarette, and have it automatically fail if I've had more than my 2 per day" (`9696710729`) · "when I go over the allocated amount it shows as completed, I'd prefer… not achieved" (`12127257673`) |

**What this adds up to:** people picture a tap as "one more of the thing I named": one glass, one bottle of 17 oz, one set of 10, one cigarette. When that one is 1, +1 is right; when it isn't, they ask for their own step ("+10", "500ml", "17oz"). Typing the exact number is the fallback for the odd amount, a missed day or a whole day at once (19 reviews). A button that says what one tap adds ("+1 glass", "+500 ml", "+10") matches all of these; a button whose behaviour depends on a rule the person can't see does not.

**Typing the habit as a sentence.** A separate scan (`nl_scan.py`) found 67 reviews asking for natural-language entry, 62 of them in to-do and calendar apps (Reminders 23, Google Tasks 14, Google Calendar 11, Microsoft To Do 7, Calendar 7) ("Move the car at 8am tomorrow… Instead you have to tap-tap-tap", `6756574716`). In habit apps it is 5 reviews: praise for goal text that is understood (`1452070582`), asks for typed dates and times (`bbb4df48-8a01-49b1-bdd9-f9bdefaa7773`), "Natural-language logging — 'ran 5k, skipped reading' parses to entries" (`14450302981`), and two complaints that an app "doesn't allow you to just type in habit, instead it quizzes you" (`dd5beccb-b430-4bfb-b97f-582dced19e32`, `f2d086e1-23dc-49e2-9672-3a215260b4a0`). **Limited evidence:** typing a sentence is wanted, but the stronger signal is §1–§6: whatever the entry method, the screen has to use the person's sentence.

## 8. Can a person's words be read automatically?

To test whether "their literal words convert into the habit", a small rule parser (`describe.py`, the prototype of the design's "type it as you'd say it" field) was run on every statement and compared with the hand codes (`crosscheck.py`).

On the 3,602 statements that describe one habit with no extras, **the parser picks the same how-often shape as the hand code for 82.6%**, and **fills in the same form rows for 85.7%** once "not said" is read as every day. Most misses are sentences that describe a habit indirectly ("I set a goal for daily steps" has no number; "my goal is 30 minutes" leaves "a day" to the reader), which is not how someone types a habit's name. Typed as sentences, the user's own examples come out right:

| Typed | How much | How often |
|---|---|---|
| "I'm trying to track my reading book habit, my weekly goal is two chapters" | 2 chapters | A week (in total) |
| "I want to track my running, 5 miles every day" | 5 miles | Every day |
| "run 8k 3 times a week" | 8k each time | 3 times a week |
| "daily, except Wednesday" | Done or not | Every day except Wednesday |
| "walk 2.6 miles 14 times this month" | 2.6 miles each time | 14 times a month |
| "no more than 3 coffees a day" | At most 3 coffees | Every day |

The full comparison, statement by statement, is `crosscheck.csv`.
