## How to read this

Every number below carries: count, percentage, denominator, scope, time window, signal label, and review IDs. Any figure resolves to exact reviews through §9.6 (theme → IDs) and §9.7 (all 67 reviews → their themes). Review IDs are the App Store's own `review_id` values and are the audit key.

### ⚠️ Seven warnings before any number here is used

1. **n = 67.** One review is 1.4925% of the corpus. Under the prescribed threshold table, a *single* reviewer already scores "meaningful" (1–3%) and *four* reviewers score "high-priority" (>5%). Those labels are printed below because the spec requires them, but at this corpus size they are **mechanically inflated**. A parallel absolute-count confidence tier is printed beside every finding (§9.2). **Never quote a percentage from this report without its count.**
2. **The rating distribution is extreme and one-sided:** 62 × 5★, 4 × 4★, 1 × 3★, **zero 2★, zero 1★**. This corpus documents why people *love* Dots. It contains almost no evidence about why anyone *leaves*. Do not read the absence of complaints as the absence of problems.
3. **No storefront reaches the 50-review threshold.** The largest is the US at 24. **Every country section in Part 6 is limited evidence and carries no standalone claim.**
4. **There is no paid tier evidenced anywhere in the corpus.** Zero reviews mention paying, subscribing, trialling, renewing or refunding *this app*. Part 5 is therefore an analysis of a *pre-monetization* product, not of buyers.
5. **Reviewers are volunteers and skew positive.** App Store review prompts typically fire after successful sessions. Silent churners are structurally invisible here.
6. **The corpus is young.** 199 days, and 50 of 67 reviews (74.63%) fall on or after 2026-06-01. Time trends in Part 7 rest on 17 / 28 / 22-review buckets and are directional only.
7. **Two reviews arrive via storefronts that do not match their language** (`14401566211` — Netherlands storefront, Spanish text). Storefront ≠ nationality. Country here means *App Store storefront*, nothing more.

## 1.5 Processing method

1. **Schema inspection** — all 13 fields enumerated and null-checked before any analysis.
2. **Reconciliation** — merged corpus vs 19 country files vs `manifest.json` (§1.3), *before* reading.
3. **Full manual read** — all 67 reviews dumped date-ordered to `temp/56-all-reviews.txt` and read one by one, **in the original language**. Spanish (8), Korean (4), Portuguese (2), Russian (1) and Traditional Chinese (1) bodies were read as written and translated at the point of quotation only. No sampling, no keyword filtering, no truncation.
4. **Hand classification** — 46 themes assigned by hand, review by review. **No automated classifier, clustering, keyword rule or LLM-batch labelling was used**, so there is no automated-classification error rate to disclose; the error mode here is single-analyst judgement, addressed by the published boundary rules in §9.1 and by the fact that every ID is listed so any assignment can be contested.
5. **Programmatic validation** — `temp/56-validate.py` asserts: zero unknown IDs, zero intra-theme duplicates, zero unassigned records, unique IDs, country-file reconciliation. **Result: PASS.**
6. **Aggregation** — counts, percentages, signal labels, mean ratings, per-theme date windows, family unions (deduplicated to unique reviewers), rating cross-tabs, period cross-tabs and storefront cross-tabs computed by script, never by hand.
7. **External corroboration** — App Store listing consulted for release notes only (§2.4); direct fetch was blocked by the network egress proxy, so external facts come from search-result snippets and are labelled lower-confidence throughout.
8. **Final verification** — every review ID appearing in this report re-checked against `reviews.jsonl` (§9.7).

## 1.6 Limitations and known biases

- **Sample size.** n=67 is small. Percentages move 1.49 points per review. Treat every sub-5-count theme as an observation, not a rate.
- **Survivorship and prompt bias.** Zero 1★ and zero 2★ reviews in 199 days is not a normal distribution for a habit tracker. Users who bounced in the first session are absent.
- **No version field.** Regression analysis by build is impossible from the corpus.
- **No purchase field, and no paid tier in evidence.** Every statement in Part 5 about monetization is about *stated preference*, never about revealed purchase behaviour.
- **Storefront ≠ market ≠ nationality.** See warning 7.
- **Translation risk.** 16 / 67 (23.88%) bodies are non-English. Nuance may be lost; originals are preserved in `reviews.jsonl` and quoted alongside translations.
- **Single-analyst classification.** Boundary calls (e.g. "easy to use" → `P_SIMPLE` vs `P_DESIGN`) are judgement. §9.1 documents each rule; §9.6 exposes every ID for challenge.
- **Recency weighting.** 74.63% of the corpus is from 2026-06-01 onward, so "the corpus view" is close to "the recent view", which suppresses the visibility of older problems.
- **External facts are lower-confidence.** apps.apple.com was egress-blocked; App Store facts here are second-hand (§2.4).

## 2.3 One apparent contradiction, resolved

Back-dating appears as both a praise and a request:

- `14226473114` (GB, 2026-06-25): *"I wish there was a feature to go back one day or two to tick my task as done."*
- `14461677979` (SG, 2026-08-23): *"it's super easy to back-log past entries."*
- `14262513041` (US, 2026-07-04): back-logging is possible but *"a little hard"* because the per-habit calendar disappeared on app re-entry.

## 3.3 Positive themes in detail

### 3.3.1 Simplicity and restraint — 39 reviewers (58.21%), high-priority, dominant, mean ★ 4.92

The largest theme in the corpus, present from the first review to the last (2026-02-20 → 2026-09-06), and the most consistent across storefronts (US 54.2%, rest-of-world 60.5%).

What makes it unusual is *how* it is phrased. Reviewers do not praise features — they praise **absence**:

- `14122888123` (DE, 5★): *"Great app that knows what it wants to do and does exactly that and nothing more."*
- `14148473814` (US, 5★): *"i like that dots does not try to do so much."*
- `13853268363` (US, 5★): *"Dots stuck because it doesn't try to do too much… zero bloat."*
- `14428584045` (US, 5★): *"no ads or unnecessary features."*
- `14472221855` (GB, 5★): *"track their habits without the faff."*
- `14440814553` (US, 5★): *"I lose interest in things if they're too complicated… This was pleasantly straight forward."*
- `14401566211` (NL storefront, Spanish, 5★): *"su simplicidad es su mayor fuerte, es imposible que te pierdas"* — "its simplicity is its greatest strength, it's impossible to get lost."

`14440814553` is the mechanism in one sentence: complexity causes this user to *abandon habit trackers*. Simplicity here is not aesthetic preference — it is the retention feature.

**Product consequence:** every request in §3.4 is a request to add something. 39 reviewers are, in effect, pre-emptively voting against that. This tension is the central roadmap question (§8.2, D1).

### 3.3.2 Free, and specifically "actually free" — 31 reviewers (46.27%), high-priority, dominant, mean ★ 4.90

Not "cheap". Reviewers repeatedly emphasise that the free-ness is **unqualified** — no trial, no cap, no gated widget:

- `14363670791` (GB, 5★): *"No obnoxious free-trial trick. Actually free."*
- `14389211332` (AR, 5★): *"realmente gratuita, sin promesas falsas"* — "really free, no false promises."
- `13982923679` (GB, 5★): *"Best free no strings app."*
- `14148473814` (US, 5★): *"an aesthetic widget that is free, which is rare to see now. free apps often have chopped widgets, or widgets just gated behind a subscription."*
- `14209620370` (US, 5★): *"its a habit tracker and its pretty dang good, best part? Its free."*
- `13897912337` (CA, 5★): *"Thank God a decent habit tracker app that is free without all the in app purchases."*

The distribution is flat across time (P1 58.8%, P2 39.3%, P3 45.5%) and across markets (high-spend group 47.1%, rest 43.8%) — this is not a price-sensitivity artefact of poorer storefronts. **US reviewers cite "free" at 45.8%, essentially identical to the 46.5% rest-of-world rate.**

### 3.3.3 Competitor paywall rejection — 9 reviewers (13.43%), high-priority, pattern, mean ★ 5.00

The acquisition mechanism, stated explicitly. These reviewers describe *leaving another app* because of its monetization:

- `14160827585` (US, 5★): *"how many apps I have had to download and instantly delete bc it has ads or a subscription. Those other apps are pretty much useless unless you pay."*
- `14151336984` (AR, 5★): *"no te pide pagar nada, tenes para ooner hábitos ilimitados, no como otras app q te piden pagar"* — "it doesn't ask you to pay anything, you can set unlimited habits, unlike other apps that ask you to pay."
- `14040460063` (US, 5★): *"Everything else similar to this app has some kind of subscription model attached."*
- `14392368857` (AR, 5★): *"sin limitarme a hábitos predeterminados o a una cantidad en específico sin antes pagar"* — "without limiting me to preset habits or a specific number without paying first."
- `14472221855` (GB, 5★): *"tried countless apps that claim to help 'neurodivergent people'… They end up being made for quick cash grabs."*

# PART 7 — TIME-TREND ANALYSIS

## 7.1 Method

The corpus spans 199 days and is back-weighted, so equal-length calendar periods would produce uneven buckets. Three periods were chosen to balance size while respecting the volume inflection at June:

| Period | Months | Reviews | % of 67 | Mean ★ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **P1 — early** | 2026-02 → 2026-05 | 17 | 25.37% | 4.824 |
| **P2 — middle** | 2026-06 → 2026-07 | 28 | 41.79% | 4.929 |
| **P3 — recent** | 2026-08 → 2026-09-06 | 22 | 32.84% | 4.955 |

⚠️ **Confidence ceiling for this entire Part.** With buckets of 17 / 28 / 22, a single review moves a rate by 3.6–5.9 percentage points. **No trend below a ~15-point swing is distinguishable from noise.** Every trend below is labelled with an explicit confidence level, and the ones marked *low* should be treated as observations to re-test when the corpus grows.

There is **no `version` field** in the schema, so no trend can be attributed to a specific build from the corpus alone. Where external release notes support an attribution (§2.4), that is stated and labelled.

## 7.2 Trend 1 — Search-driven acquisition is rising · **emerging · medium confidence**

`P_SEARCH_END`: P1 17.6% (3) → P2 17.9% (5) → **P3 31.8% (7)**.

A 14-point rise in the share of reviewers who describe *actively shopping the category* and stopping at Dots. Recent examples: `14461677979` ("Tried many apps"), `14504042664` ("tried a couple… hated them immediately"), `14518114843` ("After looking around for a free app"), `14457759095` ("La app que buscaba" — "the app I was looking for").

**Reading:** Dots is being *found by searchers* more than before, rather than discovered incidentally. This is consistent with improving store visibility. ⚠️ 3 → 7 reviewers; medium confidence.

## 7.3 Trend 2 — Ratings and volume both improving · **improving · medium confidence**

Mean ★ rose 4.824 → 4.929 → 4.955 while monthly volume roughly quadrupled from the P1 baseline (~4.3/month) to P2–P3 (~16/month). Rising volume with rising (not falling) satisfaction is the healthy pattern; the usual failure mode is the reverse.

The improvement is small in absolute terms (0.13 stars) and is driven by the disappearance of the 3★ and two of the 4★ from the recent period. ⚠️ Rating ceiling effects apply — there is very little room above 4.82.

## 7.4 Trend 3 — Two top requests were shipped mid-corpus, and the requests kept coming · **the most actionable finding in this Part · high confidence**

This is a documented arc, not an inference:

| Date | ID | Storefront | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2026-04-14 | `13958079059` | US | Requests notifications — *and hedges*: "if there is and I just haven't found it yet my apologies" |

| 2026-05-30 | `14122888123` | DE | Requests reorder |
| 2026-06-28 | `14235241668` | US | Requests per-habit notification reminders, and iPad sync |
| 2026-06-29 | `14239464502` | PH | Requests reminders |
| 2026-07-04 | `14260223015` | PH | Requests reorder |
| **2026-07-29** | **`14363670791`** | **GB** | ***"It didn't have options for reminders & shuffling the order of your habits, but it does now! -Even more happy with it."*** |
| 2026-08-05 | `14392368857` | AR | **Still requests notifications** — *"si le agregarán notificaciones"* |

**Reorder requests stopped dead** after the ship date (4 requests, all before 2026-07-29, zero after). **Notification requests did not** — two more arrived in the following three weeks.

The asymmetry is diagnostic. Reordering is visible in the list UI; a reminder setting buried in a habit editor is not. And the developer reached the same conclusion independently: the **v2.3.2 release note (2026-08-25)** describes making reminders *"easier to reach"* with a bell icon on the habit screen and on every habit row (§2.4).

**Generalised finding — this corpus's clearest transferable lesson:** Dots has a **feature-discoverability problem, not a feature-absence problem.** Three independent instances:
1. Reminders — requested twice *after* shipping (`14392368857`, `14444070845`).
2. Back-dating — requested by `14226473114` (June) while `14461677979` (August) calls it *"super easy"* and `14262513041` (July) calls it *"a little hard"* (§2.3).
3. Widget transparency — visible in the store preview, unfindable in the app (`14210943801`).

Some share of this corpus's "missing feature" requests are navigation failures. **Shipping a feature is not the same as retiring its request.**

## 7.5 Trend 4 — The sync arc: requested → broke → praised · **worsening then improving · medium confidence**

|---|---|---|
| 2026-06-28 | `14235241668` | US requests iPhone ↔ iPad sync |

Six days between the request and a data-loss report; five weeks later the same feature is the *headline* of a positive review, and specifically praised for being **optional**. ⚠️ Three reviews. The pattern is clean but n=3; the data-loss incident is n=1 and no other reviewer corroborates or contradicts it.

**Why it still matters:** it is the only evidence in the corpus of a shipped feature causing user harm, and it involves the asset (habit history) that 10 `P_OUTCOME` reviewers say is the reason they keep using the app.

## 7.6 Trend 5 — Ad-freeness praise is rising · **emerging · low confidence**

`P_NOADS`: P1 5.9% (1) → P2 14.3% (4) → P3 18.2% (4).

The app did not become more ad-free. The most plausible reading is compositional: as more users arrive by rejecting monetized competitors (Trend 1), more of them mention ads. ⚠️ 1 → 4 reviewers. Low confidence; directional only.

## 7.7 Trend 6 — Simplicity praise is persistent and slightly strengthening · **persistent · medium confidence**

`P_SIMPLE`: P1 58.8% (10) → P2 50.0% (14) → P3 68.2% (15). Never below half in any period, present in the first review of the corpus and the last. This is the most stable theme in the corpus and the safest basis for a product decision. The P2 dip is within noise.

## 7.8 Trend 7 — Free-ness praise is flat, not declining · **persistent · medium confidence**

`P_FREE`: P1 58.8% (10) → P2 39.3% (11) → P3 45.5% (10). The apparent P1 peak is 10 reviews out of 17; the absolute count is essentially constant at 10–11 per period while the corpus grew. **There is no evidence that the "it's free" novelty is wearing off** as the user base grows. Relevant to §8.2 D3: the free-ness appeal is not a launch artefact that will fade on its own.

## 7.9 Trend 8 — Defects cluster in P2 · **observation only · low confidence**

Five of the six defect/friction reports fall in P2 (Jun–Jul): `14194139904`, `14201101389`, `14210943801`, `14260223015`, `14262513041`. One falls in P3 (`14422180050`), none in P1.

Two competing readings, and **this corpus cannot distinguish them**: (a) a genuinely buggier build cycle in June–July, coinciding with the sync rollout (Trend 3); or (b) an artefact of P1's small size (17 reviews) and P3's high proportion of short praise-only reviews. Recorded as an observation, not a trend.

## 7.10 What the corpus cannot tell you about time

- **Nothing about churn over time.** No 1★ or 2★ reviews in any period.
- **Nothing about build-level regressions.** No `version` field.
- **Nothing before 2026-02-20.** Whether that is the app's launch or the crawl's horizon is not determinable from these files.
- **Nothing about retention.** Only 3 reviews are edited (`14268546710`, `14272015279`, `14354352389`), and one more carries an in-text update (`14260223015`) — four longitudinal data points in total.
- **Nothing about September.** Two reviews, both 5★. Do not read a trend into them.

## 8.2 Structural decisions

### D1 · Ship non-daily scheduling. It is the only gap with proven rating impact.

**Evidence:** `R_SCHEDULE` — 6 reviewers (8.96%, high-priority label, pattern tier), spanning the **entire corpus window** (2026-02-20 → 2026-08-11, the oldest unmet need), across 6 different storefronts (KR, GB, PH, US, AR, TW). **Sole cause of the corpus's only 3★** (`13996707039`). Still recurring at the end of the window, unlike reorder and export.

**Scope it minimally.** Five of the six requests reduce to one capability: *a habit that is not daily* — "x times per week" (`14272015279`), "once a week, once a month" (`14239464502`), "weekly/monthly/select days" (`13996707039`), "goal schedule" (`13770961885`), "daily or weekly" (`14392368857`). Ship **per-habit frequency: daily / specific weekdays / N times per week.** That is one setting.

**Do not ship the sixth as part of it.** `14414692804` asks for a *deadline* — a time-bounded goal, not a recurring habit. Different model, different UI, n=1. Keep it out (§8.5).

**The constraint:** 39 reviewers (58.21%) chose Dots because it *doesn't do too much*. This feature must be invisible to the ~58% who want daily-only — one field in the habit editor, defaulting to daily, not a new tab.

### D2 · Do not paywall anything that is free today.

**Evidence:** 35 reviewers (52.24%) volunteered praise for the absence of monetization. 9 (13.43%) name a competitor's paywall as the reason they left it. The four capabilities explicitly praised as free-unlike-competitors are **unlimited habits** (`14151336984`, `14392368857`, `14109432526`), **widgets** (`14148473814`), **all view formats** (`14472221855`) and **no ads** (9 reviewers). The appeal is **flat across high-spend and other markets** (47.1% vs 43.8%, §6.10) — it cannot be paywalled in rich storefronts without hitting the same sentiment.

This is a statement about **acquisition mechanics**, not about virtue. `P_SEARCH_END` (15 reviewers, 22.39%, rising) shows Dots winning users who are *actively shopping and rejecting paid options*. A paywall would remove the reason they stopped shopping. `14400919999` is explicit that free + widgets together is what could not be found elsewhere.

⚠️ **What this evidence does not say:** that Dots cannot be monetized, or that no user would ever pay. It says that **paywalling existing free capability attacks the documented acquisition path**, and that this corpus contains zero evidence anyone would pay for it.

### D3 · The one monetization path this corpus supports: voluntary payment.

**Evidence:** `R_DONATE` n=1 (`14444070845`, 1.49%, **single observation — weak on its own**), supported by `P_GRATITUDE` n=13 (19.40%, dominant), several addressing the developer personally, and `14067204894` calling the app *"criminally underrated"*.

**Honest framing:** one person asked to donate. That is thin, and it is stated as thin. What makes it the *only* supported path is the negative space around it — **zero reviewers asked for a premium tier, zero objected to donating, and zero would be contradicted by a tip jar**, because it gates nothing. It is the only monetization option in the option space that does not contradict D2.

**Test before building:** a tip option is low-cost and reversible. Run it as an experiment (§8.3, E1) and measure. Do not model revenue from n=1.

**A second, non-obvious asset:** `P_PRIVACY` (2 reviewers) plus the listing's "does not collect any data" plus `P_NOSIGNUP` (2 reviewers) means Dots currently monetizes *nothing* — no ads, no data, no accounts. That combination is itself a differentiator worth stating in the listing, and it is the credibility that makes a donation ask land.

### D4 · Finish the cosmetic backlog. It is cheap and it demonstrably buys stars.

**Evidence:** `R_ICONS` 5 reviewers (7.46%), `R_COLORS` 2 (2.99%), `R_DESC` 3 (4.48%). Critically, `14109432526` (ZA, 4★) states the exchange rate outright: *"i would have a 5 star rating if a few small changes could be made"* — and lists emoji, colours and descriptions. `14260223015` (4★) also lists icons among its reasons.

**Two of four 4★ reviews are cosmetic.** v2.3.0 already expanded icons to 42 (§2.4). The remaining items are **custom/system emoji** (`14109432526`, `14354352389` — both ask for arbitrary emoji, not more presets), **more colours**, and **a per-habit description field** (3 reviewers).

The description field deserves care: `14405635940` wants *"notes/comments… to help track progress"*, which is closer to journaling than labelling. Ship the **short label** first; treat journaling as a separate question (§8.4).

### D5 · Invest in widgets. It is the differentiator and the growth surface.

**Evidence:** `P_WIDGET` 9 reviewers (13.43%) name widgets as a reason to choose or stay; `R_WIDGET_MORE` 3 (4.48%) want more. Two reviewers name **free + widgets together** as the combination they could not find elsewhere (`14067204894`, `14400919999`), and `14504042664` names calendar view + widgets as the search criteria. `13853268363` describes the home-screen widget as the behaviour-change mechanism itself.

The three upward requests are specific and buildable:
- **Multi-habit widget** — `14400919999` designs it in detail: a grid with dates across the top and habits down the side, or 4 habits in one calendar widget.
- **More than 4 widgets** — `14330479445`.
- **Widget transparency** — `14210943801` (⚠️ may already exist; see F6).

### D6 · Claim the positioning the corpus already gives you.

Dots is described by its own users as: *free with no strings, simple, no ads, no account, no data collection, unlimited, beautiful, with widgets*. Every one of those is a review-derived theme with a count above 2, four of them above 9. `14472221855` supplies the sharpest line — *"With Dots, you get exactly what you download"*.

The listing (§2.4) currently leads with "simplicity, clarity, and daily consistency", which is the *category's* language. The corpus's language is **"actually free, no strings, nothing extra"** — a positioning against the category, which is how 15 `P_SEARCH_END` reviewers actually found it.

⚠️ Do **not** claim the neurodivergent/ADHD positioning on this evidence — `S_ADHD` is n=1 (§6.9), and that same reviewer's complaint is that competitors *claim* it hollowly. Earn it by staying simple, do not advertise it.

## 8.4 Research questions this corpus cannot answer

1. **Why did 24 of 43 polled storefronts return zero reviews** — Japan, France, Italy, Mexico, Turkey, Poland, Portugal, Ukraine, Thailand, Indonesia, Vietnam and 13 more — when the app claims Japanese localization? Distribution problem, listing problem, or simply low install volume outside a handful of markets? Review volume is not a downloads proxy; resolve against real install data. (§1.3, §6.11)
2. **Does an in-app purchase surface exist?** One aggregator says yes (§2.4); 67 reviewers saw nothing. Resolve from App Store Connect, not from reviews.
3. **What do the 92.5% who never review actually do?** With zero 1★/2★ reviews, churn causes are entirely invisible. Needs analytics or an in-app survey — but note that adding analytics would contradict the "does not collect any data" claim that 2 reviewers value.
4. **How many users hit the 9+ habit occlusion bug?** Depends on habit-count distribution, which reviews cannot supply.
5. **Was `14260223015`'s data recoverable, and was anyone else affected?** Requires support logs.
6. **Would the donation audience actually pay?** n=1. Only E1 answers it.
7. **Is the P2 defect cluster real or an artefact?** (§7.9) Re-test when the corpus doubles.
8. **Is the "deadline" request (`14414692804`) a real adjacent need or one user's idiosyncrasy?** n=1.

### Known boundary risks, disclosed

1. **`P_SIMPLE` is broad** (39 reviewers). It merges "minimal feature set" with "easy to use". Both were treated as the same value because reviewers consistently conflate them (`14440814553` moves between the two in one sentence). A stricter split would reduce the count; the ID list in §9.6 allows anyone to re-cut it.
2. **`M_COMPETITOR_PAYWALL` is a strict subset of `P_FREE`** — all 9 of its reviews also carry `P_FREE`. They are never summed; §3.1 uses the union.
3. **`P_DESIGN` vs `P_SIMPLE`** on words like "clean" and "깔끔" — judgement calls, both assigned when ambiguous, which *inflates* both counts slightly.
4. **`P_OUTCOME` requires a described result**, so intent-only reviews ("I'll build habits") were excluded except `14440814553`, which describes a concrete plan and setup. A borderline call.
5. **`S_INTEXT_UPDATE` n=1** means `is_edited` undercounts revisions. Longitudinal reads should use both signals.
6. **`is_edited=true` reviews show only the final text.** `14268546710`, `14272015279`, `14354352389` were classified on what is visible; their pre-edit content is unavailable.
