### ⚠️ Seven warnings before any number here is used

1. **80 reviews is a small corpus, and one review is 1.25%.** Every band in the table above is coarse at this size: a single review clears "Meaningful", four clear "Very strong". Treat any finding resting on 1–2 reviews as a lead to investigate, not a measured rate. Findings resting on 10+ reviews (design praise, the free tier, competitive preference) are the only ones this corpus can carry on its own.
2. **No storefront reaches the 50-review threshold.** The largest is the US at 14 (17.50%). **Every country in Part 6 is reported as [limited evidence] and carries no standalone conclusion.** The corpus supports global statements and cross-market comparisons of the roughest kind; it does not support "in market X, users want Y".
3. **This corpus is unusually positive, and that is itself the finding to be careful about.** 57 of 80 reviews (71.25%) are 5★ and 47 (58.75%) contain no complaint or request at all. The written mean is 4.525 against a public rating average near 4.87 in the US. Written reviews normally skew negative; here they do not, which means the usual "this is what broke" reading of a review corpus does **not** apply — but it also means the 15 negative reviews (18.75%) are carrying almost all of the diagnostic weight.
4. **The single loudest positive is a pricing decision, not a feature.** "Unlimited habits on the free plan" (`PAY_FREE_UNLIMITED`) appears in 10 reviews (12.50%) and its rate **rose from 5.0% in 2023 to 29.4% in 2025–26**. It is also a claim in the store listing. Whatever else changes, this is the thing the corpus says people chose the app for. See §2.3, §3.4 P2 and §8.3.
5. **Cross-device sync is the largest identifiable product gap and it is not mentioned in the store listing at all.** `U:sync_any` = 5 (6.25%, High-priority), mean 4.20★, and it is the stated reason for the corpus's only 3★ and two of its 4★. See §3.3 N1.
6. **4 reviewers state they paid, and three of the four describe a billing, entitlement or data failure.** Their mean rating is 2.75★ against 4.525★ for the corpus. Four people is not a rate — it is four people — but three failures out of four stated purchases is a strong enough lead that Part 5 treats it as the priority investigation, not a finding.
7. **The app has not shipped an update in ~9 months.** Version 1.4.6 dates from 2025-12-21 (external source); the corpus runs to 2026-09-02 and review volume fell from 43 in 2024 to 4 in the first eight months of 2026. Nothing in the review text says the app is abandoned — no reviewer uses that word — so this is context for reading Part 7, not a review-derived finding.

# PART 0 — READ THIS FIRST

**Executive summary in one line:** TheFor is a small, genuinely liked minimalist habit tracker whose entire competitive position rests on one pricing decision — unlimited habits on the free plan — and whose written corpus contains almost no product criticism except a short, specific and completely fixable list: no cross-device sync, a broken purchase-restore path, notifications that some users cannot turn on, and progress analytics that are thin where they aren't wrong.

## 0.1 The free tier is the product's moat, and reviewers say so explicitly

`PAY_FREE_UNLIMITED` = **10 reviews (12.50%, High-priority, mean 4.80★)**. These reviewers do not merely say the free tier is good — they name the competitor behaviour it avoids:

- `12389094071` (br, 5★) — *"a maioria dos apps permitem de 3 a 5 itens de lista e so liberam mais no premium, esse tem quantidade de hábitos ilimitados no plano free"* ("most apps allow 3 to 5 list items and only unlock more in premium; this one has unlimited habits on the free plan").
- `10550235895` (us, 5★) — *"I love that you can have as many tasks as you want which is rare for free apps."*
- `11504813604` (nl, 5★) — *"If you don't want to pay for subscription, this app will still be functional and will NOT limit the amount of habits you can add (I've been looking for this 😭)."*
- `11833253847` (fr, 5★) — *"it has unlimited habits which habit tracker apps normally dont have."*

Widen to any praise of the free offer (`U:free_tier_praise`: unlimited habits, generous free tier, no ads, no upgrade nagging) and it is **19 reviews (23.75%, High-priority, mean 4.89★)** — the highest-rated theme family in the corpus. Not one of these 19 reviews is below 4★, and 18 of them are 5★.

**The decision this supports:** the habit cap is not a monetization lever available to this product. It is the acquisition story. §8.3 shows the share of reviews naming it *tripled* over the corpus lifetime (5.0% → 9.3% → 29.4%), which is what a differentiator looks like when competitors move the other way.

## 0.2 Cross-device sync is the one thing people reduce the rating for

`U:sync_any` = **5 (6.25%, High-priority, mean 4.20★)**, of which 3 are people who tried it and found it broken rather than absent.

It is the stated reason for:

- the corpus's **only 3★** — `12454850679` (ca) — *"Love that the free version includes unlimited habits. And the paid version is a great price. However, needs to be able to sync across devices."*
- two 4★ — `12090413380` (at) *"i've tried logging on and off and deleting and reinstalling the app but nothing works :/"*; `12912139905` (ru) *"не могу синхронизировать его работу на двух устройствах — iPhone и iPad"*.
- two 5★ from the same reviewer naming it the app's notable drawback — `11057937499`, `11061269605` (de) — *"the lack of iCloud sync … a notable drawback."*

Every one of these reviewers praises the app in the same breath. **This is a gap that costs stars from people who already like the product**, which is the cheapest kind of rating to recover. The store listing does not mention sync at all, so users are not being mis-sold — they are arriving with an iPad and finding nothing.

## 0.3 Three of the four people who say they paid describe a failure

Four reviewers state a purchase (`PAY_PAID`, 5.00%, mean **2.75★** against 4.525★ for the corpus). Their stories:

| Review | Store | ★ | What they said |
|---|---|---:|---|
| `9857595761` | us | 4 | Paid annual; app still prompts for payment; **Restore Purchase does not work**; *"Unable to contact developer on their website."* |
| `11008247242` | za | 1 | *"Please remove me from your billing and unsubscribe me - I'd like this confirmed in writing"* — titled **"Phishing spam scam"**. |
| `11778005498` | in | 4 | *"clean simple UI loved it thats why purchased the pro plan"* — satisfied; wants automatic backup on Pro. |
| `12310524086` | br | 2 | *"Eu comprei o app. Desinstalei e na hora que fui instalar novamente, sumiu tudo o q estava lá e pediu novamente para comprar."* (Bought it; reinstalled; everything was gone and it asked me to buy again.) |

Two of the four (`9857595761`, `12310524086`) describe **the same underlying defect from different angles: the app does not reliably restore a purchase**. One of them lost their habit data with it. The third turned into a public accusation of fraud on a storefront where the app now averages 4.4.

Four is not a rate and this report does not treat it as one. It is, however, the highest-signal lead in the corpus: **the paid path is the only part of this product with a majority-failure account, and it is the part that generates revenue.** See Part 5.

## 0.4 Notifications: not many complaints, but the ones there are, are total

`BUG_NOTIF` = **2 (2.50%, Meaningful, mean 2.50★)** — small, but both are failures of the whole feature rather than a nuisance:

- `11037267664` (in, **1★**) — *"I am unable to receive any notifications acb you help.? It's really annoying."*
- `11830513457` (tr, 4★) — *"I unabled notificafions when I first downloaded the app and now I can not activate it on app or system notificafions."*

The second is diagnostic and cheap to act on: a user who **declines the iOS permission prompt at first run has no in-app path back**. That is a standard, well-understood onboarding bug with a standard fix (detect `denied` authorisation status, deep-link to `UIApplication.openSettingsURLString`). For a habit tracker, a user in this state has a reminder-less product.

## 0.5 Progress and analytics are the thin edge of the product

`U:progress_views_neg` (inadequate stats, wrong stats, missing charts, no per-day detail) = **4 (5.00%, Very strong, mean 3.50★)** against `TRK_OVERVIEW` praise of 4 (5.00%). The four complaints are each different and each specific:

- `10147768318` (za, 4★) — the heat map is *"quite gimmicky"*; wants to tap a day and see which habits were completed.
- `11961668175` (gb, 2★) — stats, day history and back-fill were **moved behind a subscription** (§2.4).
- `13583194808` (br, 5★) — *"só sinto muita falta de análise de dados, gráficos dos hábitos, como no aplicativo concorrente Ripples"* (I really miss data analysis and habit charts, like the competitor app Ripples) — the corpus's only named competitor.
- `13868883595` (us, **3★**) — *"Even though I completed around 75% of my test, only 20%. Why?"* — a **correctness** complaint, not a depth complaint.

The store listing promises *"Comprehensive Weekly Overview"* and *"Track Your Habit History … Gain valuable insights"*. Four reviewers, across three years and four countries, say that part underdelivers, and one says it computes the wrong number.

## 0.6 The widget request was shipped, and the corpus records the whole arc

This is the clearest cause-and-effect sequence in the corpus and the best evidence that this developer ships against review feedback:

| Phase | Window | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Absent, requested | 2023-08-31 → 2023-11-27 | `10317711703`, `10426368892`, `10550235895`, `10630906608` — 4 of the 20 reviews in 2023 (20.0%) ask for a widget |
| Shipped | between 2023-11-27 and 2024-01-02 | `10775562864` (2024-01-02) — *"there's a cute widget I can put on my home screen as the cherry on top"* |
| Praised | 2024–2025 | `WID_GOOD` = 4 (5.00%), **mean 5.00★** — `10775562864`, `11057937499`, `11061269605`, `12643553324` (*"having the free widget is awesome"*) |
| Defects found | 2024 | `11307114339` — widget won't launch the app unless the app is already backgrounded; `11792629969` — widget doesn't show already-completed tasks |
| Next ask | 2026-09-02 | `14500803900` — *"I'd like a widget that allows me to see all my habits and their progress and allows me to check them off"* — i.e. **interactive** widgets |

`REQ_WIDGET` goes 20.0% (2023) → **0.0%** (2024) → 5.9% (2025–26). The zero is the shipped feature. The 5.9% is a different, larger ask.

## 0.7 The one monetization backlash, and why the corpus does not corroborate it

`11961668175` (gb, **2★**, 2024-11-17) is the only reviewer who says value was taken away:

> *"app fell off when you introduced a paid subscription to be able to view stats of habit tracking or just not even able to see which days were tracked, and to try and track previous days u might've forgotten to … You prided yourself on offering a great app with absolutely no payment or prices for subscription for stats … Now you're just like everything else."*

This is `PAY_PAYWALL_REGRESSION` = 1 review (1.25%, Meaningful *by band only* — see warning 1). **The corpus does not support generalising it**, and there is direct counter-evidence: in the 20 reviews written **on or after** that date, free-tier praise runs at **40.0% (8/20)** and unlimited-habits praise at **25.0% (5/20)** — both higher than in the 60 reviews before it (18.3% and 8.3%). Whatever moved behind the paywall in late 2024, **it was not the habit cap**, and the free offer people actually talk about survived intact.

The honest reading: one long-tenured user lost a specific capability (stats + back-fill) and left. That is a retention story worth one engineer-day of investigation, not a strategy reversal.

## 0.8 Two revenue lines are completely invisible in the corpus

The store listing (accessed 13 Sep 2026) carries six in-app purchase lines: **Pro Month $1.99, Pro Year $9.99, Pro Year With Trial $9.99, Pro Lifetime $29.99, Routine AI $2.99, and "Routine" at $0.99 / $1.99 / $2.99.**

Searched across all 80 reviews: **zero** mention AI in any form, and **zero** mention buying a routine or routine pack. The Explore/preset-routine surface appears exactly three times, and only one of those is positive:

- `11293453970` (eg, 4★) — *"I am struggling to find the section of exploring popular routines"* — cannot find it.
- `12643553324` (pt, 5★) — *"It would be nice if there was more content in the explore part"* — it is too thin.
- `12489656823` (ng, 4★) — *"You can even copy habits that are already in the app and add them to your own routine"* — the only person who found it useful.

A paid content catalogue that no reviewer has ever mentioned buying, attached to a surface that one reviewer cannot locate and another finds empty, is either pre-revenue or mis-placed. Absence of evidence in 80 reviews is weak evidence — but it is *uniformly* weak in one direction.

## 0.10 The seven decisions this corpus supports

1. **Ship cross-device sync.** 5 reviews, mean 4.20★, every one of them otherwise positive. Largest identifiable gap. (§3.3 N1)
2. **Fix restore-purchase and make it impossible to lose data on reinstall.** Two of four stated payers hit this; one lost everything. (§0.3, Part 5)
3. **Never cap free habits.** It is the single most-cited reason for choosing the app, and its citation rate is rising. (§0.1, §8.3)
4. **Add a recovery path for denied notification permission.** One 4★ describes being permanently locked out of reminders. (§0.4)
5. **Fix, then deepen, analytics.** One reviewer says the percentage is *wrong*; three say it is too shallow. Correctness first. (§0.5)
6. **Ship an interactive widget with check-off.** The widget's own success created the next ask. (§0.6)
7. **Decide whether the Routine / Routine AI catalogue is a business.** Zero reviews in 3.5 years mention it; one reviewer cannot find the surface it lives on. (§0.8)

## 1.2 Schema actually present

All 13 fields are present on all 80 records, with **zero empty titles and zero empty bodies**:

`review_id` · `app_id` · `app_name` · `country` · `country_name` · `rating` · `title` · `body` · `author` · `date` · `vote_count` · `vote_sum` · `is_edited`

Bodies arrive HTML-escaped; the pipeline unescapes before any reading or matching.

**Fields that do not exist in this corpus and therefore cannot be analysed:** app version per review, device, developer response, helpfulness ranking beyond the raw `vote_count`. Any claim in this report about *when* something shipped is inferred from review text and dates, or taken from the external store listing — never from a version field, because there isn't one.

## 1.4 Deduplication rule

**No records were deduplicated.** There are **zero exact `(title, body)` duplicate pairs** and **zero repeated review bodies**. The denominator for every global percentage in this report is therefore 80.

One author name appears twice: **`ShuyinD` (de) posted `11057937499` on 2024-03-18 and `11061269605` on 2024-03-19** — near-identical 5★ text, one day apart, second version dropping the icon complaint and adding *"the developer reacts quickly to feedback"*. Both are retained and both are counted, because they are distinct records on the storefront and the second contains information the first does not. They are flagged `META_DUP_AUTHOR` so any theme they both carry can be re-checked at n−1. The affected themes are `DES_SIMPLE`, `DES_UI`, `WID_GOOD` and `REQ_SYNC`: at n−1, `WID_GOOD` falls from 4 to 3 (5.00% → 3.75%) and `REQ_SYNC` from 5 to 4 (6.25% → 5.00%). **Both stay above the 3% "Very strong" line, so no finding in this report depends on counting that pair twice.**

## 1.5 Processing method

1. `69-dump.py` — unescape, sort by `(date, review_id)`, assign working index 1–80, emit `69-all-reviews.txt`, `69-chunks/`, `69-index.tsv`.
2. Read all four chunks in order, in full. Record one line per review in `69-cls/001-080.txt` (`index CODE,CODE` plus a free-text note). The codebook grew while reading; codes introduced late were applied backwards by re-reading the affected reviews.
3. `69-themes.py` — the codebook: **62 codes in 13 families**, plus **15 composite unions**. Definitions in Appendix B.
4. `69-build-classification.py` — resolve index → `review_id` against `reviews.jsonl` and emit `69-review-classification.py`. Asserts on any duplicate index, out-of-range index, undefined code, repeated code within a review, or unclassified record.
5. `69-validate.py` — integrity gate (§1.3 last four rows, plus: zero undefined codes, zero unused codes, all union members defined, rating distribution matches manifest). Output at `69-validate-out.txt`.
6. `69-aggregate.py`, `69-extra.py`, `69-checks.py` — counts, unions, ratings, storefronts, market groups, eras, language, integrity, and targeted verification of every narrative claim made in Parts 0 and 8.
7. `69-tables.py` — generates every table in this report into `69-gen/`, plus `_facts.json`.
8. `69-build-report.py` fills the numeric placeholders and refuses to write if any placeholder is unresolved; `69-verify-report.py` re-checks the written file against the corpus (§1.8).

**Classification is entirely manual.** No automated clustering, no keyword rule, no model-assigned label was used to place a review in a theme. Keyword search was used only in `69-checks.py`, and only to test **negative** claims (§0.8: "no review mentions AI") where a false negative would be the dangerous error.

## 1.6 Limitations — read these before citing any figure

1. **Size.** 80 reviews. One review = 1.25%. The signal bands in the "How to read this" table were designed for corpora an order of magnitude larger; at this size a "Meaningful" label can mean one person. Where a finding rests on 1–2 reviews this report says so in the sentence, not only in the band.
2. **No eligible country.** The 50-review threshold is met by nobody (max: us, 14). Part 6 contains **no standalone country claims**.
3. **Selection.** The 80 written reviews are 30% of the 267 public star ratings across the same 23 storefronts. The other 70% rated without writing and are unrepresented here. Thailand is the sharpest case: 38 public ratings, 4 written reviews.
4. **Positive skew, direction unknown.** Written corpora usually skew negative relative to the rating base; this one does the opposite in some markets (za: written mean 2.50 vs public 4.40) and matches in others. With 80 records there is no way to tell whether that is solicitation, small-sample noise, or a genuinely contented user base. **No claim in this report depends on the skew resolving one way.**
5. **No version field.** Cause-and-effect claims (the widget shipping, §0.6) are inferred from the text and dates of reviews plus the external listing. They are stated as inference, and the inference is shown.
6. **Language.** 17 reviews (21.25%) are not in English. All were read in the original; translations in this report are the analyst's and are marked. Machine translation was not used.
7. **Three years, thin at the ends.** 2023 has 20 reviews across 10 months, 2026 has 4 across 8 months. Month-level trend statements are not attempted; only the three-era comparison in Part 7, and that one is reported with its own caveats.
8. **External data is a snapshot.** Prices, version and language support were read on 13 Sep 2026 and are marked as such. They describe the app *now*, not at the time any given review was written — which matters most for §2.4, where a 2024 reviewer describes a paywall that cannot be verified against a 2026 listing.

## 2.4 The one structural ambiguity in the model

`11961668175` (gb, 2★, 2024-11-17) states that viewing stats, seeing which days were tracked, and back-filling forgotten days all moved behind a subscription. The 2026 listing still advertises weekly overview and habit history as headline features, and `11669968530` (in, 2024-08-30) praises *"good stats"* three months earlier without mentioning a paywall.

**This report does not resolve the contradiction and does not pretend to.** Three readings fit the evidence: a paywall was added and later relaxed; a paywall was added to *depth* while a basic view stayed free; or one user hit a bug. What the corpus does establish (§0.7) is that the free **habit cap** was never introduced, because praise for unlimited free habits rises after this date rather than falling. **This is the single highest-value thing to check against the actual entitlement configuration**, because the product's whole positive story (§0.1) depends on the free tier's reputation.

- `11700911557` (in) — *"Buy this app asap"*, and `11255993177` (vn) — title *"Must buy"*: advocacy, no reason given.

**The pattern across all five:** nobody names a Pro feature as the reason. They buy because the free product earned it. That is a **goodwill-funded** monetization model, and it is fragile in a specific way — it depends on the free tier staying good and the developer staying visible, which is exactly what §0.7 and N5 put at risk.

## 5.4 Upgrade barriers — why people don't buy

The corpus contains almost **no** stated refusal to upgrade, which is itself the finding. What it contains instead:

1. **Nothing to upgrade *for*.** The free tier is uncapped and does the job (§0.1). `12192328429` says it outright: *"no es necesario pagar"* — it isn't necessary to pay. `11771810441` is a rare reviewer who names premium as worth having, and does not say what is in it. **The corpus contains no description of a single Pro feature.** Across 80 reviews and four purchasers, nobody says what Pro gives them.
2. **The one thing a Pro user asked for is not in Pro.** `11778005498` bought Pro and then asked for auto-backup *on the Pro plan* — i.e. the paid tier does not contain the feature its own buyer most wanted.
3. **`PAY_NO_NAG`** — `10798954077` praises the *absence* of upgrade prompts. Whatever the conversion cost of that restraint, it is currently earning 5★ reviews.

**The strategic problem this poses:** a product whose free tier is its moat has no obvious upgrade ladder that does not damage the moat. The corpus offers one way out — §8.3 M2.

## 5.5 Post-purchase problems, refunds, cancellation

| ID | Problem | Status in the corpus |
|---|---|---|
| `9857595761` (us, 4★, 2023-04-24) | Paid annual; app keeps prompting for payment; **restore purchase fails**; developer unreachable | Unresolved as written |
| `12310524086` (br, 2★, 2025-02-14) | Reinstall wiped data **and** the entitlement; asked to buy again | Unresolved as written |
| `11008247242` (za, 1★, 2024-03-04) | Wants to cancel and be removed from billing; wants written confirmation | Unresolved as written |

**No refund is described as granted or refused anywhere in the corpus.** No reviewer says Apple refunded them. One (`11008247242`) is seeking cancellation, which is an Apple-side action the developer cannot perform — but the fact that the user did not know that, and used a public 1★ to ask, is a product-surface failure the developer *can* fix.

The two entitlement failures are **22 months apart** (2023-04 and 2025-02) and on different storefronts, which argues against a one-off release regression and for a persistent weakness in the receipt-validation / restore path.

## 5.6 What would change a paying user's mind

Directly from the four payer reviews and the five intent reviews, in order of what the corpus supports:

1. **Make restore work, and prove it in-app.** Both entitlement failures (§5.5) would have been prevented by a reliable restore and a visible "you are Pro" state.
2. **Put backup in Pro.** The only feature a Pro buyer asked for (`11778005498`), and it is also the mitigation for `12310524086`'s data loss. One feature closes both payer complaints.
3. **Give Pro a describable benefit.** Nobody in 80 reviews can say what Pro contains. Purchase intent here is goodwill (§5.2); goodwill converts better when there is something to point at.
4. **Do not fund Pro by taking from free.** §0.7 shows the one attempt cost a multi-year user; §0.1 shows the free tier is the acquisition engine.

## 7.4 The four trends this corpus does support

### T1 — The free tier became the reason people review — **rising, strongly**

`U:free_tier_praise`: **5.0% (1/20) → 23.3% (10/43) → 47.1% (8/17)**. `PAY_FREE_UNLIMITED` alone: **5.0% → 9.3% → 29.4%**.

Nearly half of everything written in the last 20 months is about the free offer. Two readings fit and they are not exclusive: the app's positioning sharpened, or **the category tightened its free caps around it** and made an unchanged policy newly remarkable. The corpus supports the second indirectly — reviewers increasingly describe the competitor behaviour rather than just the app's (`12389094071`, 2025: *"a maioria dos apps permitem de 3 a 5 itens"*; `13583194808`, 2026: *"o único aplicativo que encontrei que não limita"* — the only app I found that doesn't limit).

**This is the most decision-relevant trend in the report.** The moat is getting more valuable, not less.

### T2 — The widget shipped — **a completed request cycle**

`REQ_WIDGET`: **20.0% (4/20) → 0.0% (0/43) → 5.9% (1/17)**, with `WID_GOOD` appearing only from 2024-01-02. Full timeline in §0.6. The 2026 reappearance is a different, larger ask (interactive check-off), not a regression.

This is the corpus's only clean shipped-feature arc, and it is evidence for something Part 8 depends on: **this developer reads reviews and acts on them.**

### T3 — Defect reports are rising as a share — **rising, on small numbers**

`U:bugs_any`: **5.0% (1/20) → 11.6% (5/43) → 23.5% (4/17)**. In absolute terms that is 1 → 5 → 4 reviews.

The E3 four are sync (`12912139905`), data loss (`12310524086`), sync again (`12454850679`) and analytics correctness (`13868883595`). **The share rise is partly arithmetic** — as the low-information praise reviews dry up (`META_LOWINFO` 20.0% → 20.9% → **5.9%**), the people still writing are the ones with a problem. But the absolute count did not fall while volume fell by two-thirds, and that is the part worth acting on.

### T4 — Sync emerged as a theme and has not gone away — **rising**

`U:sync_any`: **0.0% (0/20) → 7.0% (3/43) → 11.8% (2/17)**. Absent for the app's entire first year, present in every year since. Consistent with a user base that has aged into owning two devices, and with the absence of any sync feature to age into.

## 7.5 What persisted, and what this corpus cannot say

**Persistent across all three eras:** design praise (65.0% → 39.5% → 52.9% — a dip in the middle era driven by 2024's unusually high share of one-line reviews, not a fall in regard), competitive preference (25.0% → 25.6% → 17.6%), reported outcomes (15.0% → 14.0% → 17.6%), and monetization complaints (5.0% → 4.7% → 5.9% — **flat, and always one review per era**).

**Improving:** nothing measurable. No theme reverses from negative to positive over the corpus except the widget request, which was fixed rather than improved.

**What the trend data does not support, stated explicitly:**
- **No decline in product quality.** The rating drift is inside noise and the defect mix changes rather than deepens.
- **No dating of the paywall change.** §2.4's contradiction cannot be resolved by era data — one review is one era-point.
- **No seasonality.** 35 months, 80 reviews, max 6 in any month.
- **No release-linked analysis of any kind**, because the corpus has no version field (§1.2) and the external listing gives only the *current* version date.
- **No claim about 2026.** Four reviews (`13583194808` 5★, `13868883595` 3★, `14189467986` 5★, `14500803900` 5★, mean 4.50★) is a sample, not a year.

1. **What is in Pro?** Not one of 80 reviews describes a Pro feature, including from the four people who bought it. This is the largest blind spot in the report.

2. **F3** — notification permission recovery. One 1★, and the fix is a known iOS pattern.
3. **F6 + F7** — the goal picker and an in-app support link. Both are hours, not days, and both have named reviewers waiting.

Then **B1 (sync)**, which is simultaneously the largest gap (§3.3 N1), the best candidate for Pro's first describable benefit (M2), and the theme rising fastest among defects (§7.4 T4).

# PART 9 — EVIDENCE APPENDIX

## A. Counting rules

1. **Denominator.** Every global percentage uses **80**, the full corpus. No review is excluded from any global denominator for any reason — not low information, not non-English, not the duplicate-author pair.
2. **Non-exclusive themes.** A review may carry several codes; 245 assignments over 80 reviews, mean 3.06 per review. Percentages across a family therefore sum to more than the family total.
3. **Unions count each review once.** `U:` codes are set-unions over member codes; a review carrying three members of a union contributes 1 to it.
4. **Signal bands** are applied to the share of the **full corpus**, never to a sub-denominator. Where a sub-denominator is useful (payers, 4★ reviews), the figure is explicitly labelled a **segment rate**, both denominators are shown, and the band is not restated.
5. **Mean ★ per theme** is the unweighted mean rating of the reviews carrying that code. With n = 1–4 it is quoted for completeness and carries no statistical meaning.
6. **Direction** (`pos` / `neg` / `req` / `mixed` / `fact`) is a property of the **code**, not of the review that carries it. A `neg` code inside a 5★ review is still negative — see §4.8.
7. **Dates** are the review's own `date` field, UTC, truncated to the day. Eras are calendar years (§7.1).
8. **Review IDs** are the source `review_id` verbatim. Every ID printed anywhere in this report is verified present in `reviews.jsonl` (§1.8).
9. **External facts** (price, version, languages, release date) come from the iTunes Lookup API and App Store listing accessed 13 Sep 2026, are labelled at every point of use, and are never mixed into a review-derived count.

## C. Judgement calls and known error risk

Every borderline decision made while classifying, so a reader can re-derive or overturn any of them:

1. **`12310524086` is classified as data-loss + restore-failure, not sync**, despite its own title being *"Problemas com sincronização"*. The described event is a reinstall that lost local data and re-presented a paywall; no second device appears. Reclassifying it as `BUG_SYNC` would move `U:sync_any` from 5 to 6 (7.50%) and `U:data_continuity` is unaffected (it already includes both). **§3.3 N1's argument does not depend on this call.**
2. **The `ShuyinD` pair (`11057937499`, `11061269605`) is counted twice.** Rationale and the n−1 sensitivity check are in §1.4. No finding changes band.
3. **`PRAISE_GEN` and `META_LOWINFO` overlap but are not the same code.** `PRAISE_GEN` means "positive with no identifiable feature"; `META_LOWINFO` means "too short to carry detail". A review can be one without the other: `11833253847` (fr) is short and unspecific *and* names unlimited habits, so it carries `PRAISE_GEN` but not `META_LOWINFO`.
4. **`11008247242` ("Phishing spam scam") is coded `PAY_BILLING` + `TRUST_NEG` + `PAY_PAID`, not fraud.** The text demands unsubscription; it does not describe an unauthorised charge. `PAY_PAID` is assigned because being billed implies a purchase. This is the most interpretive call in the corpus.
5. **`9857595761` is coded `META_CONTRA`.** A reviewer describing a failed paid subscription and an unreachable developer awarding 4★ is a genuine mismatch. Flagged, not adjusted.
6. **`11293453970` ("struggling to find the section of exploring popular routines") is `UX_FIND`, a negative, not `REQ_EXPLORE_CONTENT`.** The feature exists (`12489656823` used it); the reviewer could not locate it. The distinction matters: one is a build item, the other is a navigation fix.
7. **`REQ_SYNC` is assigned to the two de reviews that call iCloud sync a "drawback" but do not report trying it.** `BUG_SYNC` is reserved for the three who describe two devices failing to match. Both are reported separately throughout.
8. **`11961668175` carries `DES_UI`** even though it is a 2★, because the text praises the UI explicitly. This is the clearest illustration of rule A.6.
9. **Codes with n = 1 are retained rather than merged.** 27 of the 62 codes appear once. Merging them would inflate apparent signal; dropping them would hide 22% of the corpus's requests. They are reported at n = 1 with the count visible.
10. **Known error risk.** Classification is one analyst's judgement on 80 reviews, applied in a single pass with backward re-reads when codes were added. The highest-risk codes are the three interpretive ones — `PRAISE_GEN`, `META_LOWINFO` and `TRUST_NEG` — and the design trio (`DES_SIMPLE` / `DES_UI` / `DES_EASE`), where reviewers' wording often spans two of the three. **No finding in Parts 0, 3, 5 or 8 rests on the design trio's internal split**; they are reported both individually and as the `U:design_praise` union for exactly this reason.
